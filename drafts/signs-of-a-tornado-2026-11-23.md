# Signs of a Tornado: What You Can See and Hear (and Why You Shouldn't Wait)

Knowing the signs of a tornado is useful. Waiting for those signs before you act is not. By the time a tornado is clearly visible or loud enough to hear over the storm, you may have very little time left, and some tornadoes never show you a classic funnel at all. Think of the signs below as extra warning on top of the official alerts, never as a replacement for them.

That's especially true in Texoma, where trees, hills, rain and darkness often hide what's coming. Here's what to watch and listen for, what the signs don't tell you, and what to do the moment you notice one.

## Signs you can see

Forecasters and storm spotters look for a handful of visual clues. You might notice some of them from your yard or car.

**A rotating wall cloud.** A wall cloud is a lowered, often blocky cloud base that hangs beneath the main storm, usually on the rain-free side. A wall cloud by itself isn't a tornado. What matters is whether it's rotating, and whether the rotation is strong and lasts. Slow, shapeless, broken cloud movement is common in thunderstorms. Persistent, tightening rotation is the part that worries forecasters.

**A funnel cloud.** A funnel is a rotating, cone-shaped cloud that hangs down from the storm. If it hasn't reached the ground, it's technically a funnel cloud, but it can reach the ground quickly and without warning. Treat any funnel as a reason to move.

**Whirling dust or debris at ground level.** This one surprises people. A tornado is a rotating column of air that's in contact with the ground, and the air itself is invisible. What you see is the condensation funnel and the dirt and debris it picks up. If you see a swirl of dust, leaves, insulation or pieces of buildings under a cloud base, a tornado may already be on the ground, even if you can't see a funnel above it.

**A low, dark cloud that seems to be moving fast and close to the ground.** Sometimes the tornado is hidden inside a wide mass of cloud or a thick curtain of debris. The National Weather Service describes tornadoes that are wrapped in rain or surrounded by debris so you can't clearly see the funnel at all.

**Hail or heavy rain followed by an abrupt change.** Large hail is common in storms that produce tornadoes, but it also falls from plenty of storms that don't. If heavy rain or hail suddenly gives way to dead calm, or to a fast, intense wind shift, take that seriously.

## Signs you can hear

Sound is often the first sign at night and in heavy rain.

- **A loud, continuous roar or rumble.** People often compare it to a freight train or a jet engine. The key difference from thunder is that thunder rises and fades. A tornado's roar keeps going.
- **A sudden calm.** If the wind and rain stop abruptly during a severe storm, don't walk outside to look. The calm can come right before a rapid shift as the storm's circulation passes.
- **Debris rattling or crashing.** The sound of things hitting the house or yard in a way that doesn't match ordinary wind.

At night, there's one more clue the National Weather Service mentions: small, bright blue-green or white flashes near the ground. These are typically power lines or transformers being damaged. If you see them near a storm, treat it as a serious warning. We cover more in our guide to [nighttime tornadoes](/blog/nighttime-tornadoes/).

## What the signs don't prove

Some "signs" get repeated so often that people trust them more than they should.

- **A greenish sky.** You'll hear that a green sky means a tornado is coming. It isn't reliable. A green tint can show up in storms without tornadoes, and tornadoes can happen without it. It may mean a storm is intense, but it isn't a tornado alarm.
- **Large hail.** It often appears in severe storms, but it isn't proof.
- **A calm or quiet sky.** Tornadoes can form with little advance sign overhead, and some arrive with no visible funnel.
- **Not seeing a funnel.** Absence of a visible funnel is not absence of a tornado. Rain, dust, trees and darkness hide them.

For more on beliefs that can get people hurt, see [tornado myths](/blog/tornado-myths/).

## Why you shouldn't wait to see one

Tornadoes can move quickly, and their paths can change. A storm that looks distant may be close. Visibility drops in heavy rain. At night you can't see a thing. And in Texas, where the land opens into long views but is also dotted with trees and low rolling hills, what you see from your porch isn't necessarily what's heading your way.

Official alerts are built to reach you before you notice any of this. A tornado watch means conditions could produce tornadoes, and a warning means one has been spotted or indicated by radar. For the difference, see [tornado watch vs warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/). Wireless Emergency Alerts also send warnings to phones in the warned area with no app or sign-up, which we explain in [tornado alerts on your phone](/blog/tornado-alerts-on-your-phone/). Radar can show rotation before anyone sees it, which is part of how warnings go out in time. If you're curious how that works, see [how to read tornado radar](/blog/how-to-read-tornado-radar/).

The rule is simple. If a warning is issued, move now. If you see or hear a sign and no warning exists yet, move anyway.

## What to do when you notice a sign

1. Go to your shelter or the safest place you have right away. Don't stop to film it.
2. If you're in a house with no basement, get to the lowest floor, into an interior room like a closet or bathroom, with as many walls as possible between you and the outside. Our article on [the safest place in a tornado](/blog/safest-place-in-a-tornado/) goes through the options.
3. Stay away from windows. Cover your head and neck.
4. If you're in a vehicle, don't try to outrun it unless it's clearly far away. See [what to do in a tornado while driving](/blog/what-to-do-tornado-while-driving/).
5. Stay put until the warning expires or officials say it's safe.

## Tornado signs in Texoma specifically

Spring, roughly March through June, is the main tornado peak in North Texas, with a smaller bump in the fall. But tornadoes occur here in every month. Rain-wrapped tornadoes happen here too, and in a heavy downpour you may not see one at all. Many homes here sit on slabs with no basement, so there's no below-grade place to retreat to quickly.

If you live near the lake, the signs may be harder to spot. Water, shorelines and trees can break up views. For more on that, read [Lake Texoma tornado safety](/blog/lake-texoma-tornado-safety/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does a tornado sound like?

Most people describe a loud, continuous roar, similar to a freight train or a jet engine. Unlike thunder, it doesn't rise and fade. If you hear it, go to shelter immediately.

### Does a green sky mean a tornado is coming?

Not reliably. A green tint can appear in intense storms, but it also shows up without tornadoes, and tornadoes happen without it. Don't use sky color as your alarm. Watch alerts and warnings instead.

### Can a tornado happen without a visible funnel?

Yes. A tornado may be hidden by rain, dust, darkness or a mass of debris. If you see whirling debris under a cloud base, treat it as a tornado even if no funnel is visible.

### What is a wall cloud?

A wall cloud is a lowered, often blocky cloud base beneath a thunderstorm. It becomes a concern when it rotates strongly and persistently. A wall cloud alone isn't a tornado, but strong rotation is a reason to take cover and check for warnings.

### Is it calm before a tornado?

Sometimes. A sudden drop in wind or rain during a severe storm can occur as the storm's circulation passes. It's not a safe sign. If a tornado warning is active, stay in shelter until it expires.

### Should I wait to see a tornado before going to shelter?

No. Visibility can be poor, and tornadoes can move quickly. When a warning is issued or you notice a sign, go to your shelter or the safest place available right away.

# NOAA Weather Radio in Grayson County: What to Buy, How to Program It, Where to Keep It

Your phone will probably warn you about a tornado. A siren might, too, if you're outside and the wind is right. A NOAA weather radio is the third layer, and it's the one that doesn't depend on a cell tower, a data connection, or whether you remembered to charge anything. It sits on a nightstand, waits, and goes off when the National Weather Service issues a warning for your county.

This guide covers why a weather radio is worth the small cost, how to choose one, how to program it for Grayson, Fannin, Cooke and the Oklahoma counties across the lake, and how to test it so you know it works before the season starts.

## Why a weather radio when you already have a phone

A weather radio does one job. It listens to a National Weather Service broadcast and wakes you up when something dangerous is headed your way. That narrow focus is its strength.

Phones are great, but they fail in ordinary ways. They're on silent, in another room, face down in a charger, or switched to Do Not Disturb. Cell service can get congested or knocked out during a storm, and a dead battery at 2 a.m. is not a plan. Our guide to [tornado alerts on your phone](/blog/tornado-alerts-on-your-phone/) covers how to set the phone side up properly, and you should. But a radio gives you a second, independent path.

Three things make a weather radio different:

- **It's tied to the NWS warning system.** The alert comes from the same forecast office that issues the warning. For Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties, that's the Fort Worth office (NWS Dallas/Fort Worth).
- **It's loud on purpose.** The alert tone is designed to wake a sleeping household, and most radios add a flashing light.
- **It works when other things don't.** Battery backup means a power outage doesn't silence it, and it doesn't need a cell signal.

If you've read our breakdown of [tornado watch vs warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/), the radio is how you hear the warning when you're asleep, in the shower, or running a loud TV.

## What to look for when you buy one

You can spend a little or a lot. A basic radio from a hardware or big-box store does the essential job. Here's what actually matters.

**SAME programming.** SAME stands for Specific Area Message Encoding. It lets you tell the radio which counties you care about, so it only sounds for those. Without it, a radio alerts for every warning its transmitter covers, which in a big coverage area means a lot of alarms for storms that are nowhere near you. That trains people to ignore the radio, which is the worst outcome. Choose a model that supports SAME.

**Battery backup.** Look for a radio that plugs into the wall but also runs on batteries when the power drops. Storms knock out power often, and that's exactly when you need the radio most. Keep fresh batteries in it and swap them on a schedule. Some models also have a hand crank or solar panel, which is a nice extra but shouldn't replace real batteries.

**A real alert tone and a light.** The radio should have a distinct, loud alarm and ideally a visual flash. If anyone in your home is hard of hearing, look for models that support a strobe, bed shaker or other add-on.

**Alert filtering.** Better models let you choose which kinds of alerts sound the alarm. At a minimum, make sure tornado warnings will always trigger it. Many people also leave severe thunderstorm warnings on, since those can carry damaging wind and large hail.

**Decent reception.** A weather radio picks up a signal from a transmitter, so a model with a telescoping antenna or an external antenna jack helps in houses with metal siding, thick walls, or a rural location.

You don't need an expensive radio. You need one that's programmed correctly, powered reliably, and placed where you'll hear it.

## Find your local transmitter

Weather radios receive broadcasts from NOAA transmitters, and each transmitter serves a specific area. Rather than guess, look up the NWS NOAA Weather Radio station listing for Texas, find the transmitter nearest to your address, and use the information it gives you to tune your radio. The listing also shows which counties each transmitter covers.

## The SAME county codes for the Texoma area

This is the part that trips people up, so take it slowly. Your radio's manual will explain how to enter codes, but the codes themselves are the same for everyone. Each county has a six digit SAME code. For our area:

| County | State | SAME code |
|---|---|---|
| Grayson | Texas | 048181 |
| Fannin | Texas | 048147 |
| Cooke | Texas | 048097 |
| Collin | Texas | 048085 |
| Bryan | Oklahoma | 040013 |
| Marshall | Oklahoma | 040095 |
| Love | Oklahoma | 040085 |

A few notes on using them:

- **Program your own county first.** If you live in Sherman, Denison, Pottsboro or Van Alstyne, that's Grayson, 048181. If you're in Bonham, it's Fannin, 048147. Gainesville is Cooke, 048097.
- **Add the neighbors you travel through.** If you work in Collin County, or family is across the Red River, add those codes too. Most radios let you store several. Just keep the list sensible. Every code you add is another county that can wake you up.
- **Remember the Oklahoma side.** Bryan, Marshall and Love counties are served by the NWS Norman office, not Fort Worth. If you live near the lake or the river, adding the Oklahoma county next to you means you'll also hear about warnings on that side. For more on this, see our guide to [Lake Texoma tornado safety](/blog/lake-texoma-tornado-safety/).
- **Check the code against the NWS source.** Codes are standardized, but typos happen. Compare what you entered to the official county list before you trust it.

## Programming it, step by step

Every model has a slightly different menu, so keep the manual handy. The general order looks like this:

1. Plug it in, install backup batteries, and set the clock if the radio asks.
2. Run the channel scan to find the strongest NOAA signal.
3. Enter your county's SAME code, then any others you want.
4. Choose which alert types sound the alarm. Keep tornado warnings on, at a minimum.
5. Set the volume high enough to wake you from another room, but not so high you'll disable it out of annoyance.
6. Confirm the radio is set to alert or standby mode, not just listen. Some radios stay quiet until the alert mode is turned on.

## Testing it, and how often

A radio you haven't tested is a radio you're hoping works. Most radios have a test button that checks the alarm, tone and light. Press it. Then confirm the sound carries to your bedroom with the door closed.

The National Weather Service also runs routine tests of the alerting system on a regular schedule, and your radio should respond if your county code is programmed. Ask your county emergency management office, or check the NWS Fort Worth site, for the routine test schedule. If your radio doesn't react during a scheduled test, recheck the SAME code, the signal strength and the alert settings.

A simple routine works well:

- Test the alarm when you start spring storm season, and again before the smaller fall peak. North Texas has tornadoes in every month, so don't assume it's only a spring concern. Our [Texas tornado season guide](/blog/texas-tornado-season/) explains the pattern.
- Replace backup batteries once a year, on a date you'll remember.
- Retest any time you move the radio, change batteries or lose power for a long stretch.

## Where to put it, and why one should live in your shelter

Placement matters as much as programming. Put the main radio in or near the bedroom, where it can wake you. A kitchen counter or a living room shelf is fine for daytime, but a tornado at 3 a.m. is the scenario you're planning for. If you have a two story home or a large house, a second radio near the other sleeping area is worth considering.

Then think about the shelter itself. If you have a safe room or storm shelter, a battery powered weather radio inside it does two jobs:

- It tells you when the warning has expired or when a second round is coming, so you aren't guessing whether it's safe to open the door.
- It keeps working if power and cell service are down, which is common after a severe storm.

Radio reception can be weaker inside a steel or concrete unit, especially underground, so test it with the door closed. If the signal is poor, an external antenna or a placement near the door or vent may help. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I still need a weather radio if I have a smartphone?

Yes, it's worth having both. Phones get silenced, run out of battery, lose service, or sit in another room. A weather radio is a separate path to the same National Weather Service warning, and it's built to wake you up. Treat it as a backup that costs very little.

### What SAME code do I use for Grayson County?

Grayson County's SAME code is 048181. For nearby counties, Fannin is 048147, Cooke is 048097 and Collin is 048085. Across the Red River, Bryan County, Oklahoma is 040013, Marshall is 040095 and Love is 040085. Enter your own county first and add others only if you need them.

### How often should I test my NOAA weather radio?

Press the test button when you set it up, and check it again at the start of each storm season. Replace the backup batteries once a year. You can also watch for the routine NWS alert system tests, and if your radio doesn't respond during one, recheck your county code and signal strength.

### Where do I find my local NOAA transmitter?

Use the NWS NOAA Weather Radio station listing for Texas. It shows which transmitter covers your area and which counties it serves. Most radios can also scan for the strongest signal automatically. If reception is weak at your address, try a different room or an external antenna.

### Will a weather radio work inside a storm shelter?

Often, but not always. Steel and concrete block radio signals, and underground shelters are the hardest. Test the radio inside with the door closed before storm season. If the signal is poor, try an external antenna or a different spot near the door or vent.

### Should I program more than one county into the radio?

Add your own county, plus any you regularly travel through or where family lives. Keep the list short, because every code you add is another county that can trigger an alarm. If you live near the lake, the Oklahoma county next to you can be worth adding, since storms don't stop at the Red River.

# How Deep Is an Underground Storm Shelter?

It's one of the first questions people ask, and it's reasonable. If you're going to put a shelter in the ground, you want to know how far down it goes. The short version: there's no single depth that works for every shelter. The depth is set by the manufacturer's engineering for that specific model, and what matters more than any number is whether the shelter is designed, tested and installed correctly for your soil and your site.

Here's how to think about depth, what changes it, and what to ask before anyone brings out an excavator.

## Depth comes from the design, not a rule of thumb

You'll see people online quoting a "standard" depth, as though digging to a certain number guarantees safety. It doesn't work that way. A tested underground shelter is a complete engineered system: the shell, the lid or door, the anchoring, the backfill and often a drainage plan. The depth is one output of that design, not an input you pick.

Different shelters sit differently. Two common designs for residential use:

- **Slope-front (walk-in) shelters.** These have a sloped or stepped entry on one side. The shelter is partly below grade, and you walk or step down to a door. Our [walk-in hillside shelter guide](/blog/walk-in-hillside-storm-shelter/) covers how that style works on a sloped lot.
- **Flat-top shelters.** The roof sits flush or nearly flush with the ground surface, and you enter through a hatch or lid with stairs or a ladder below. The structure is buried deeper, since the whole shell is under the surface.

A flat-top design needs more excavation than a slope-front, because the entire box goes down. In 2026 pricing for North Texas, an underground concrete slope-front typically runs $5,200-$7,800 installed, and a flat-top runs $5,800-$8,800. The price reflects excavation, the shell and the install work, so depth is one of the things that adds up.

If a salesperson gives you a depth in feet, ask where it comes from. The right answer is the manufacturer's installation specification for that model. If you want a number for planning, check the model's spec sheet, not a general guideline.

## What affects how a shelter is set in the ground

Several site conditions change the engineering, and a good installer checks them before quoting.

### Soil type

Much of Grayson County sits on Blackland Prairie clay, which shrinks when it dries and swells when it gets wet. Clay moving against a buried structure creates pressure, and it can shift a shell over time if the design doesn't account for it. Cooke County is mostly Cross Timbers country, with different soils again. Ask the installer how the shelter is designed to handle the soil at your address, and whether they've checked it before quoting.

### The water table and drainage

Groundwater is the big one. A buried shelter is a hollow box, and a hollow box in wet ground wants to float. This is called buoyancy, and it's why manufacturers specify how a shelter is anchored and what the backfill and drainage look like. A high water table, or a lot that collects runoff, changes the plan or rules out an underground unit entirely.

Ask directly:

- Has anyone checked the water table and drainage at my site?
- How does the design resist floating, and how is it anchored?
- Where does water go if it collects around or in the shelter?
- What happens after several days of heavy rain?

If your yard floods or stays soggy after storms, say so up front. An [above-ground safe room](/above-ground-safe-rooms/) or a [garage floor shelter](/garage-floor-shelters/) may fit better. Our comparison of [above-ground vs underground shelters](/blog/above-ground-vs-underground-shelter/) lays out the tradeoffs, and our [guide to water in a storm shelter](/blog/water-in-storm-shelter/) covers what to do if moisture is a problem.

### Utilities and lot layout

Before any digging, someone calls 811. It's free, and it gets buried utilities marked: gas, water, electric, sewer, communications. Hitting a line is dangerous and expensive, and it's also avoidable. Whoever does the digging normally places the 811 request. Confirm your installer has done it and that the marks are down before the excavator starts.

Beyond utilities, the lot itself matters. The shelter needs access for equipment, room for the excavation and a location with a clear route from the house. Septic tanks and drain fields, trees and their roots, property setbacks and the slope of the yard all affect where a unit can go. Check with your city or county building department about permits and setbacks, and see our guide to [storm shelter permits in Texas](/blog/storm-shelter-permits-texas/).

## Why deeper isn't automatically safer

It's tempting to assume a deeper hole means a safer shelter. In practice, the shelter's strength comes from how it's built and tested, not from how much dirt is on top of it.

A shelter built to ICC 500 and FEMA P-320 criteria is designed for a 250 mph wind speed in North Texas, with a debris test using a 15 lb 2x4 at 100 mph. That test applies to the walls, roof and door. An underground shelter can earn those ratings at the depth the manufacturer specifies. Digging deeper than the spec doesn't make it stronger, and it can create new problems: more soil load, more water exposure, a longer climb out, and a design that no longer matches the tested one.

Think of it this way. Changing the burial depth on your own is like changing the specs on a tested part. You've stepped outside what the testing covered. Our overview of [ICC 500 safe room requirements](/blog/icc-500-safe-room-requirements/) explains what the standard actually covers.

## Getting in and out matters as much as depth

Depth isn't just a structural question. It affects how fast you can get in, and how you get out.

A slope-front shelter has a short walk or a few steps. A flat-top has a hatch and a stair or ladder down. For anyone with mobility limits, small children or older family members, those stairs matter. If getting down takes too long in the dark, with a warning on, then the design isn't working for your household. Our guide to [wheelchair-accessible storm shelters](/blog/wheelchair-accessible-storm-shelter/) covers options if stairs are a problem.

Think also about the way out. Debris can land on top of a hatch or in front of a door, and you need a plan for opening it from inside. Ask the installer to show you how the exit works and what happens if something heavy sits on top of it. See our guide to [storm shelter doors](/blog/storm-shelter-doors/) for more on latches and hatches.

Finally, plan for the time you'll spend inside. A buried shelter needs ventilation, and your [emergency kit](/blog/emergency-kit-storm-shelter/) should be stored where you can reach it. Our guide to [storm shelter ventilation](/blog/storm-shelter-ventilation/) explains how air gets in.

## A checklist of questions for an installer

Bring these to every quote. The answers tell you how seriously someone takes your site.

| Question | What a good answer sounds like |
|---|---|
| What depth does this model require, and where is that written? | The manufacturer's installation spec for the specific model |
| Has anyone checked soil, water table and drainage at my address? | Yes, and here's what it means for the design |
| How does the shelter resist floating in wet ground? | Anchoring and backfill described in the spec |
| Who calls 811 and when? | Whoever digs places the request ahead of time and confirms marks before excavating |
| Is the whole system tested to ICC 500, including the lid or door? | A test report or statement of compliance for that model |
| How do I get out if debris lands on the entry? | A clear, demonstrated method |
| What happens if you hit rock or groundwater during digging? | A written plan and how the price changes |
| Is the quote in writing, with everything included? | Yes: excavation, haul-off, backfill, permits, finish work |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How deep do you have to dig for an underground storm shelter?

It depends on the model. The manufacturer's installation specification sets the depth for that design, and a flat-top sits deeper than a slope-front. Ask for the spec for the exact model, and don't rely on a general number you find online.

### Is a deeper storm shelter safer?

Not by itself. Safety comes from a design that's been tested to ICC 500 and installed to the manufacturer's spec. Going deeper than specified can add soil load, water exposure and a harder exit, and it can move the installation outside what the testing covered.

### Can I put an underground storm shelter in clay soil?

Often yes, but the design and installation need to account for it. Blackland Prairie clay in much of Grayson County swells and shrinks with moisture, which affects the pressure on a buried shell. Ask the installer how the model and the backfill handle clay at your address.

### What happens if the water table is high at my property?

A hollow shelter in wet ground can try to float, so the design needs anchoring and drainage for it. A high water table or a lot that holds runoff may rule out an underground unit. In that case, an above-ground safe room or a garage shelter may be the better choice.

### Do I really need to call 811 before installing a shelter?

Yes. Calling 811 gets buried utilities marked before anyone digs, and it's the expected step before any excavation. Whoever does the digging normally places the request, so confirm your installer has and that the marks are down before work starts.

### Which is deeper, a flat-top or a slope-front shelter?

A flat-top is generally buried deeper, since the whole shell sits below the surface with the roof near ground level. A slope-front is partly below grade with a sloped entry on one side. Check the exact dimensions in the manufacturer's spec for the model you're considering.

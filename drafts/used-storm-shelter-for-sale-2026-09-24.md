# Storm Shelters for Sale: What to Check Before You Buy New or Used

Search "storm shelter for sale" and you'll get three different kinds of listings mixed together: new units sold by a dealer without installation, floor-model or lot units ready to haul, and used or pre-owned shelters sold by individual owners on marketplace sites. The sticker price on any of these rarely covers what you'll actually spend, and a used shelter that looks fine in photos can be hiding damage that matters the day you need it. Check these before you commit to any unit, new or used.

## What "For Sale" Usually Covers

A storm shelter listing generally falls into one of three buckets. The first is a new unit sold by the manufacturer or a dealer, priced for the shelter itself, with delivery, site work, and setup quoted separately. The second is a lot or floor unit a local dealer already has on hand, often the same deal but with a shorter lead time. The third is a used shelter, above-ground or underground, sold privately after a homeowner moves, upgrades, or sells a property.

None of these prices is the number you'll pay to have a working shelter on your property. Installation is a separate job with its own labor, equipment, and site conditions, and it's the part that varies most from one property to the next. Before you get attached to a listing price, get a written estimate that accounts for your actual site: soil type, access, and distance from the road all change what the job costs.

## What the Sticker Price Usually Leaves Out

A few line items tend to be missing from a "for sale" number, and they're the ones worth asking about before you get attached to a price. Delivery is its own cost and it scales with distance and access, since getting a multi-ton steel or concrete shelter from a lot or a seller's property to yours isn't cheap. Above-ground units and heavy underground shells also need to be set with a crane or forklift, not muscled into place, and tight side yards or overhead lines only make that harder. Then there's the ground itself: an above-ground unit needs a slab rated to hold it and anchor into, while an underground or garage in-floor unit needs excavation, backfill, and drainage work if it goes below grade.

- **Anchoring.** The shelter has to be bolted to something engineered to hold it in place under wind and debris load, not just sitting on grade.
- **Permit.** Many cities require a permit for a storm shelter installation, separate from the unit purchase; ask your local building department.
- **Electrical, if any.** A light or a vent fan means a circuit, which is its own line item if your shelter includes one.

None of these are figures we'll put a number on here, because they depend on your property and the specific unit. ## What to Verify on Any Unit Before You Buy

Whether you're looking at a new floor model or a used shelter listed by a previous owner, a few things are worth checking before money changes hands. A seller who can't answer these, or won't let you look, is a reason to keep shopping.

| What to Check | Look For | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| ICC 500 test report | A report or manufacturer statement tied to the exact model and manufacturer, not a general claim | A test report for a different model doesn't apply to the unit in front of you |
| Manufacturer's label | A permanent data plate with model number, manufacturer, and date of manufacture | Confirms the unit is a standard build, not a rebadged or altered one |
| Door assembly | Door, frame, hinges, and latch working as one set, with no bent frame or forced closure | ICC 500 tests the door as a complete assembly; a good door on a bent frame won't perform the same |
| Anchor holes and hardware | Clean, round holes with intact threads, no elongation or shear marks | Torn or stretched holes suggest the unit was pried loose from a slab rather than unbolted |
| Rust and corrosion | Surface rust is common and often cosmetic; rust-through or flaking at welds is not | Rust that's eaten through metal weakens the shell exactly where it's meant to hold |
| Weld seams | Continuous, even welds, with no visible cracks or grinding marks covering a repair | A cracked or reground weld may no longer perform as the tested design assumed |
| Shell condition | Dents, cracks, or flex in walls or roof, especially near lift points | Removal from a slab or from the ground is where shells usually pick up damage |
| Interior hardware | Bench anchors, vents, and any wiring intact and not corroded | Confirms the unit is complete, not missing parts that were tested as part of the whole |

If a seller can produce the test report and the unit passes a walk-around against this list, that's a reasonable sign. If they can't, or the unit has clearly been pulled out of the ground or off a slab without much care, treat the price as a starting point for negotiation, not a done deal.

## Buying a Used Above-Ground Shelter: Plan on New Anchors

An above-ground steel safe room that's been unbolted and moved needs to go back down the same way it went down the first time: bolted into a slab engineered to hold it, with anchors sized and placed for that unit. You can't reuse the old anchor holes from a different slab, and a shelter set on an existing patio or garage floor that wasn't built for it isn't a shortcut worth taking. Read up on how [anchoring systems](/blog/storm-shelter-anchoring-systems/) actually work and what [the installation process](/blog/storm-shelter-installation-process/) involves before you assume a used unit will simply bolt down wherever you put it. Check the [door assembly closely](/blog/storm-shelter-doors/) too, since a door that's been through one install and removal already is more likely to show the wear that matters.

## Why Used Underground Units Carry More Risk

Underground shelters, whether concrete or fiberglass, are harder to inspect and harder to move safely, which makes a used one riskier than a used above-ground unit. Removal usually means digging out around the shell and lifting it, and that's when hidden cracks form or existing ones widen, especially in shells that were installed in expansive clay soils common across Grayson County. Buoyancy is another concern: in saturated ground, a lighter shell that isn't properly anchored or ballasted can shift or heave, and a used unit's original anchoring may not have accounted for your soil. If you're looking at a used [fiberglass unit](/blog/fiberglass-storm-shelters/) specifically, get underneath it and inspect the shell for stress marks before you buy, not after it's back in the ground. For a side-by-side on what underground units require compared to above-ground, see our [underground storm shelter page](/underground-storm-shelters/).

## Rebate Considerations Before You Buy Anything

If a Grayson County rebate is part of your budgeting, get the order of operations right: it reimburses 50% of the installed cost, up to $3,000, but only if you apply and get approval before installation, not after, and that holds for a used shelter the same as a new one. Check current funding and eligibility with the [county's program page](https://www.co.grayson.tx.us/page/oem.saferoom) or our [rebate breakdown](/blog/grayson-county-storm-shelter-rebate/) before you buy anything. Fannin and Cooke County homeowners shouldn't assume the same program applies.

## New or Used: Matching the Purchase to Your Situation

A new unit costs more up front, but it comes with manufacturer's paperwork, no mystery about how it was handled before it reached you, and nothing to second-guess on installation day. A used unit can save money if it checks out on the inspection points above and the seller can produce the test report, but the savings can disappear fast if it needs new anchoring hardware, a slab modification, or turns out to have shell damage a seller didn't disclose. There's also a decision buried in most "for sale" browsing that's less about new versus used and more about design: prefab units sold off a lot versus a shelter built to fit your specific site and household size. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I buy a storm shelter without installation?

Yes, most dealers sell the shelter itself separately from installation. Just know the delivery, slab work, anchoring, and permit are typically quoted on top of that unit price, not included in it, so ask for a full installed estimate before comparing listings on price alone.

### Is a used storm shelter safe to buy?

It can be, if you verify the ICC 500 test report matches the exact model, check the door assembly and shell for damage, and confirm the anchor holes weren't torn out during removal. A used unit with no documentation and visible shell damage is a harder sell no matter how low the price is.

### Can I move a storm shelter to a new house?

Above-ground units can usually be relocated, but they need new anchors set into a slab engineered for that specific shelter at the new address. Underground units are riskier to move because removal is where cracks and shell damage most often happen.

### Does a used shelter still need a permit?

If your city requires a permit for a storm shelter, it applies to the installation on your property, so a used unit is treated the same as a new one. Ask your local building department.

### Will a used shelter qualify for the Grayson County rebate?

It can, but only if you apply and receive approval before the installation happens, the same as with a new unit. The home also has to sit outside the mapped 100-year floodplain and the shelter has to meet FEMA and ICC 500 criteria, so check with the county's Office of Emergency Management before you buy.

### What's the real difference between a "storm shelter" and a "tornado shelter" listing?

Nothing structural. Sellers and dealers use the two terms interchangeably for the same category of product, whether it's an above-ground steel safe room, an underground concrete shelter, or a garage in-floor unit. What matters is the specific model's test report and condition, not which term the listing uses.

# When Is Tornado Season in Texas? A North Texas and Texoma Calendar

Texas doesn't have one official tornado season, but if you had to draw a line around it, spring holds it. Activity statewide builds through April, peaks in May, and tapers off by the end of June. North Texas, including Texoma, also sees a smaller second bump in the fall. Outside those stretches a tornado is less likely here, but it's never off the table.

## The Texas Tornado Season, in Plain Terms

If you're searching "texas tornado season" or "north texas tornado season" looking for a straight answer, here it is: the spring peak runs roughly April through June, and May is typically the busiest month of the three. That's when the ingredients for severe weather line up most often across the state, Texoma included.

North Texas has a second, smaller season too. Fall brings another stretch where the atmosphere sets up for tornadoes, though it's usually a quieter version of spring, not a repeat of it. Between those two windows, and even in the dead of winter, a tornado can still happen. Treat spring and fall as your highest-alert stretches, but stay ready the other months too. The calendar doesn't guarantee anything.

## Why Texoma Sits Where It Does on the Map

Texoma, the stretch of North Texas anchored by Sherman and Denison along the Red River Valley, sits in a spot where the atmosphere regularly sets up for severe storms. Warm, moist air moves up out of the Gulf and runs into drier air and cold fronts pushing down from the north and west. Where those air masses collide, and where wind shear is strong enough to organize a storm, you get the kind of setup that can produce a tornado.

That's the same basic recipe that runs through the broader region people call Tornado Alley, a belt that stretches from Texas up through the Plains. Texoma sits near its southern edge, which is part of why "tornado alley texas" turns up alongside "north texas tornado season" in the same searches. It doesn't mean every storm becomes a tornado. It means the region's geography and weather patterns give tornadoes more chances to form here than in a lot of the country. The [Storm Prediction Center](https://www.spc.noaa.gov/) is the federal office that tracks and forecasts that severe weather risk across the whole belt, Texoma included.

None of that changes what's under your feet, either. Most homes in Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties are built slab-on-grade with no basement, largely because of expansive Blackland Prairie clay that shrinks and swells, shallow limestone in places, and water tables that make basements costly to waterproof. That's one reason a purpose-built shelter, rather than "the basement," is how most Texoma households actually plan for this.

## Storms Tend to Build Late in the Day, and Some Arrive After Dark

Severe thunderstorms in Texoma commonly develop and intensify in the late afternoon and evening, once the day's heat has had hours to build instability in the atmosphere. That's why a lot of watches and warnings in this region land between roughly mid-afternoon and midnight. Checking the forecast once in the morning and moving on doesn't cut it on an active-weather day. Keep half an eye on the sky through the evening instead.

Not every tornado waits for daylight to end, either. Some of the most dangerous setups carry into the overnight hours.

### Why Night Tornadoes Are More Dangerous

A tornado after dark takes away the two things people rely on most: their eyes and their sleep schedule. You can't see a wall cloud or a funnel forming in the dark the way you can in daylight, so a warning on your phone or a weather radio becomes the only real heads-up. And if the warning comes while you're asleep, there's no margin for hesitation. Understanding the difference between a [tornado watch and a tornado warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/) ahead of time, so you're not looking it up at 2 a.m., is one of the simplest things a Texoma household can do to close that gap. For the full breakdown straight from the source, [NWS explains watches versus warnings here](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-ww).

## Who Issues the Alert, and What to Do Before You Have a Shelter

Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties are served by the National Weather Service office in Fort Worth. A tornado watch, issued by the Storm Prediction Center, means conditions favor tornadoes across a broad area for several hours. A tornado warning, issued by NWS Fort Worth, means a tornado has actually been spotted or indicated by radar in your immediate area. A watch is "get ready." A warning is "take shelter now."

If you don't yet have a dedicated safe room or storm shelter, NWS guidance is to get to the lowest floor of the house, an interior room away from windows, and cover your head. Mobile and manufactured homes are not considered safe in a tornado even when tied down; the guidance is to leave for a sturdier building or shelter before the storm arrives, not during it. If that describes your situation, it's worth reading up on [mobile home storm shelter options](/blog/mobile-home-storm-shelter/) before you need one. Don't shelter under a highway overpass, and don't try to outrun a tornado in a car. Neither is considered safe.

## A Season-by-Season Planner for North Texas Homeowners

Treating storm-shelter planning as a spring scramble is a common mistake. The calendar below breaks the year into the stretches that actually matter for decisions, not just for weather-watching.

| Time of Year | What's Happening | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Winter (Dec-Feb) | Tornado activity is at its quietest for the year, though not zero. | Best window to install a shelter without a wait. Good time to research and apply for the Grayson County storm shelter rebate if you're eligible, since approval has to come before installation. |
| Early Spring (Mar) | Severe weather starts becoming more frequent as the peak approaches. | Last comfortable stretch to get a shelter installed or inspected before things pick up. Walk your family through where to go and how fast you can get there. |
| Peak Season (Apr-Jun, May busiest) | The most active stretch of the year for Texoma and North Texas generally. | Keep a way to receive watches and warnings at all hours, including overnight. Know your shelter's location without thinking about it. Keep basic supplies inside it. |
| Summer (Jul-Aug) | Activity eases compared to spring, but severe storms still happen. | Good time for a mid-year check of your shelter's door, seals and anchoring, and to restock anything used during spring. |
| Fall (Sep-Nov) | North Texas gets its smaller secondary season. | Don't treat this as an off-season. Keep the same watch/warning habits as spring, just at a lower simmer. |

That fall-and-winter window matters for another reason: storm shelter installers around here tend to get busiest in the weeks right after a spring outbreak, when demand jumps all at once. Planning ahead in the fall or winter, rather than waiting until storms are already in the forecast, is the more comfortable way to go about it. It gives you time to compare [above-ground safe rooms](/above-ground-safe-rooms/) against other options, understand [what the installation process actually involves](/blog/storm-shelter-installation-process/), and get a written quote instead of a rushed decision.

## Putting the Winter Window to Use

Winter and early fall are also when it's realistic to look into the [Grayson County storm shelter rebate](/blog/grayson-county-storm-shelter-rebate/), run through the county's Office of Emergency Management with FEMA and state funding. Approval has to happen before installation starts, and it's Grayson County only, funding rounds open and close on their own schedule, so check the county's page rather than assume you qualify. Fannin and Cooke County homeowners should ask their own emergency management office whether anything similar is running.

## Off-Season Downtime Is Good for More Than Waiting

The quieter months aren't just for installing a shelter. They're also when it's easiest to:

- Confirm your shelter's anchoring and door still close and latch the way they should.
- Restock a basic [emergency kit](/blog/emergency-kit-storm-shelter/) for the shelter itself, not just the house.
- Review, with everyone in the household, exactly where the shelter is and how you'll get to it if a warning hits at night.
- Get a written estimate on your own timeline instead of a compressed one, and check current [storm shelter cost ranges](/storm-shelter-cost/) for your situation before spring pushes demand up.

None of that requires waiting for a watch to be posted. It's easier to think through calmly in January than during a warning in May.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When does tornado season start in Texas?

There's no single official start date. Activity typically begins increasing in April, builds through May, and eases off by the end of June. That's the closest thing Texas has to a defined spring tornado season.

### Is there a tornado season in North Texas specifically?

North Texas follows the statewide spring pattern, roughly April through June with May as the busiest month, and also sees a smaller secondary season in the fall. Tornadoes can still happen outside both windows.

### What month has the most tornadoes in Texas?

May is generally the most active month statewide, sitting in the middle of the spring peak that runs from roughly April to June.

### Can tornadoes happen in Texas outside of tornado season?

Yes. Tornadoes have been recorded in Texas in every month of the year. The safest approach is year-round readiness, not a seasonal on/off switch.

### Why do North Texas tornadoes often happen in the evening or at night?

Storms here commonly build through the afternoon as the day's heat adds energy to the atmosphere, so they often intensify in the late afternoon and evening. Some setups carry into overnight hours, which is more dangerous because it's harder to see an approaching storm and easier to miss a warning while asleep.

### Does living in Tornado Alley mean Texoma sees more tornadoes than other parts of Texas?

Texoma sits near the southern edge of the broader region called Tornado Alley, where warm Gulf air regularly meets drier air and fronts. That geography gives the area frequent chances for severe weather, but it doesn't mean every storm season looks the same or that every storm produces a tornado.

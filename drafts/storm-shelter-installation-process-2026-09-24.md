# Storm Shelter Installation: Step by Step, From Site Visit to First Storm

Storm shelter installation follows the same basic sequence no matter which unit you pick: a site visit, soil and utility checks, any required permit or rebate paperwork, delivery, then setting the shelter and anchoring or backfilling it in place. What changes is how long each stage takes and how much ground gets disturbed, and that mostly comes down to whether you're getting an above-ground safe room, a garage in-floor shelter, or an outdoor underground unit.

## What Happens Before Anything Gets Installed

Every job starts with a site visit. An installer walks the property, looks at the spot you have in mind (an existing garage slab, a patio, a corner of the yard), and takes measurements: door swing clearance, ceiling height if it's going in a garage, distance to the house, and access for a delivery truck or excavation equipment. For [above-ground safe rooms](/above-ground-safe-rooms/), this is mostly about slab condition and floor space. For [underground storm shelters](/underground-storm-shelters/), the visit also covers grade, drainage direction, and how close the dig site sits to the foundation, a fence line, or a septic system.

This is also when you'll talk through which type fits your household size, your budget, and your lot. If you haven't settled on a type yet, the [storm shelter cost guide](/storm-shelter-cost/) breaks down the price ranges for above-ground, underground, and garage units side by side.

## Slab, Soil and Utility Checks

Before anyone digs or anchors, the ground itself gets checked. For an above-ground unit going onto an existing slab, that means confirming the slab is level, in good condition, and thick enough to anchor into. For anything that involves digging (underground shelters and most garage in-floor installs), the crew needs to know what's down there first.

By Texas law, you have to call 811 (Texas811) before any excavation, and that call has to happen before digging starts, not after. Texas811 notifies the utility companies with lines near your property, and they mark buried gas, electric, water, and communication lines before the crew breaks ground. This isn't optional and it isn't a formality: hitting a buried line mid-dig is expensive, dangerous, and it will stop the job cold.

Much of Grayson County sits on Blackland Prairie clay, which is part of why [basements are rare here in the first place](/blog/why-texas-homes-dont-have-basements/): the clay shrinks and swells with moisture, which makes traditional basement waterproofing costly and unreliable. That same clay behavior matters for underground shelters too. In saturated ground, a lightweight shell can be pushed upward by water pressure if it isn't properly anchored or ballasted, which is one more reason the soil and drainage assessment happens before anything gets set in the ground.

## If You're in Grayson County: Handle the Rebate First

Grayson County's Residential Safe Room Rebate Program can reimburse 50% of the installed cost, up to $3,000, for an eligible single-family home, but you have to apply and get an award letter before installation, not after, and the home has to sit outside the mapped 100-year floodplain. Funding rounds open and close, so confirm current availability with the [county's program page](https://www.co.grayson.tx.us/page/oem.saferoom) or our [rebate guide](/blog/grayson-county-storm-shelter-rebate/) before you count on it. Fannin and Cooke County homeowners should check with their own office and budget on the full installed price until they know.

## Permits: Check Before You Book Delivery

Whether you need a permit depends on your city and, sometimes, on whether the shelter is above-ground or buried. Rules differ between cities and between city limits and unincorporated county land, so ask your local building department what applies to the unit you're planning. Either way, this is a step to clear before delivery gets scheduled, not something to sort out afterward. Our [Texas storm shelter permit guide](/blog/storm-shelter-permits-texas/) covers what each area in Texoma typically requires.

## Delivery Day

Prefabricated above-ground units, underground shelters and garage in-floor units typically arrive on a flatbed or specialized delivery trailer. The truck needs a clear path to the install site, which is why the site visit covers access for equipment, not just the shelter's footprint. Overhead lines, narrow gates, and soft ground can all change how a delivery gets handled.

## Setting the Shelter: Three Installation Paths

The setting process is where the three shelter types really diverge.

### Above-Ground Safe Room on an Existing Slab

An above-ground unit gets positioned on the prepared slab and anchored directly into the concrete with the anchoring hardware specified for that shelter. It's a bolting and leveling job, not an excavation job, which is why above-ground units are usually the quickest and simplest of the three to put in.

### Garage In-Floor Shelter

A [garage floor shelter](/garage-floor-shelters/) sits flush with the garage slab, so this path involves cutting and excavating a pit inside the garage footprint, setting the shelter unit into that pit, then backfilling and finishing the surrounding slab so the shelter sits flush and the garage floor stays usable for parking. Utility checks matter here too, since garage slabs sometimes have plumbing or electrical runs beneath them.

### Outdoor Underground Shelter

An underground shelter (concrete slope-front or flat-top) is excavated to depth, set into the hole, leveled, and then backfilled in stages so the surrounding soil compacts properly around the unit rather than settling unevenly later. The site gets graded afterward so water drains away from the shelter opening instead of pooling against it. This is the most ground-intensive of the three installation types, and weather and soil conditions on the day of the dig can affect how it goes.

## Doors, Vents and the Final Walkthrough

Once the shelter is set and anchored or backfilled, the installer checks the door for proper alignment, smooth operation, and a secure seal against the frame, along with the latching hardware. This matters because a door is only as good as its installation: ICC 500 testing evaluates a door as a complete assembly (door, frame, hinges, and latches together), and a well-built door that's hung poorly doesn't give you the protection the assembly was tested for. Vents get checked for clear airflow and correct placement too. You can read more about how doors get evaluated in our [storm shelter doors guide](/blog/storm-shelter-doors/).

The installation closes with a walkthrough: how the door and any secondary latch work, what a clean install looks like around the shelter's edges, and what to expect if you're waiting on backfill settling or a final grading pass. This is a good time to ask questions before the crew leaves, not after the first storm.

If you already have a shelter and want to know whether it's holding up rather than how a new one goes in, that's covered separately in our [storm shelter inspection process guide](/blog/storm-shelter-inspection-process/), which walks through what to check on an existing unit rather than what happens during a new install.

## Registering Your Shelter

Once the shelter is in and ready, some Texoma-area emergency management offices offer voluntary registration, so first responders know a shelter is on the property in case anyone needs to be found after a storm. It takes a few minutes, and where it's offered, do it. Our [guide to registering your storm shelter](/blog/register-your-storm-shelter/) covers how that works locally.

## How Long Installation Actually Takes

We won't put a number of hours or days on this, because it depends on soil, weather, permit timing, and the specific unit, and any contractor who promises an exact timeline before seeing your site is guessing. What we can tell you in relative terms: above-ground and garage in-floor units are usually the quickest to install, since there's less ground to move. Outdoor underground shelters take the longest, mainly because of excavation, backfill compaction done in stages, and weather, since wet or frozen ground slows digging and backfill work down. For a fuller breakdown of what drives timeline and cost, see our [storm shelter installation cost factors guide](/blog/storm-shelter-installation-cost-factors/).

## What to Do Before the Crew Arrives

A little prep on your end keeps installation day moving. For a garage in-floor shelter, clear the whole bay, not just a spot on the floor: tools, storage and parked vehicles need to be out of the way. Make sure there's a clear path from the street or driveway to the install site for a delivery truck and any excavation equipment, including gate width and overhead clearance. And if you know where irrigation lines run near the dig site, mention it during the site visit so the crew can route around them.

- **Plan for pets.** Digging equipment, open trenches, and unfamiliar crews on the property don't mix well with dogs or cats loose in the yard. Keep them inside or elsewhere on install day.
- **Ask about the rebate and permit timing.** If either applies to you, get them lined up before you book delivery. Both need to be settled before installation, not during it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take to install a storm shelter?

It depends on the type and your site conditions, so we won't quote hours or days. Above-ground and garage in-floor units are generally the quickest since they involve little or no excavation. Underground shelters take longer because of digging, staged backfill, and weather.

### Do I need a permit to install a storm shelter in Texoma?

It depends on your city and sometimes on the shelter type. Rules differ between cities and unincorporated county land, so ask your local building department. Check before you schedule delivery. Our [Texas storm shelter permit guide](/blog/storm-shelter-permits-texas/) has the details by area.

### Is it true I have to call 811 before installation?

Yes, if excavation is involved. Texas law requires a call to Texas811 before any digging, and the utility companies then mark buried lines on your property. This has to happen before the crew breaks ground, not after.

### Can I install a storm shelter myself?

Most installations, especially underground and garage in-floor units, involve excavation, anchoring, and drainage work that's difficult to get right without the right equipment and experience. A poorly installed shelter, particularly a poorly hung door, may not give you the protection the shelter itself was built for.

### What's the difference between this and a storm shelter inspection?

Installation is what happens when a new shelter goes into the ground or onto a slab for the first time. Inspection is a separate process for checking an existing shelter's condition, door function, and drainage over time. See our [storm shelter inspection process guide](/blog/storm-shelter-inspection-process/) for that.

### Does the Grayson County rebate cover installation costs?

It can reimburse up to 50% of the installed cost, capped at $3,000, for an eligible single-family home in Grayson County. You have to apply and receive approval before installation begins, and the home must be outside the mapped 100-year floodplain. Fannin and Cooke County homeowners should ask their own county emergency management office whether anything similar is running.

# Tornado Myths: What You've Heard That the National Weather Service Says Isn't True

Almost everyone grew up with a few tornado "rules." Open the windows. Hide in the southwest corner. Drive under an overpass. Some of them were taught in schools, repeated by relatives, or shown in movies. The National Weather Service has spent years telling people that many of these ideas are wrong, and some are dangerous.

Here are the most common tornado myths, what the guidance actually says, and what to do instead. If you live in Grayson, Fannin or Cooke County, where tornadoes can happen in any month, it's worth getting these straight before the next watch is issued.

## Myth: Open the windows to equalize pressure

This one is old and stubborn. The idea was that a tornado's low pressure would make a sealed house explode, so you should open windows to let the pressure out.

The NWS says this doesn't work and it's a waste of time. Damage to houses comes from violent winds and flying debris, not from a pressure difference. Time spent walking around opening windows is time you're not getting to shelter, and you're moving toward glass at the worst possible moment. Leave the windows alone, stay away from them, and go straight to your safe spot.

## Myth: An overpass is a good place to shelter

Videos of people surviving under overpasses have spread this idea. The guidance is the opposite: an overpass is a bad place to be.

The structure can funnel wind and debris and make conditions worse underneath it. You're exposed to flying objects, and there's nothing protecting you from the sides. It also traps traffic, which puts other drivers and first responders in harm's way. Our guide on [what to do in a tornado while driving](/blog/what-to-do-tornado-while-driving/) covers the options when you're on the road. The short version is to get into a sturdy building if you can, and never treat a highway bridge as a shelter.

## Myth: The southwest corner of the basement is safest

You may have been taught that tornadoes approach from the southwest, so one particular corner of the basement is the place to be (which corner depends on who told you). Tornadoes can come from any direction, and storm motion varies. A particular corner isn't reliably safer.

What matters is being on the lowest level, in a spot protected by as many walls as possible, away from windows, and ideally under something sturdy like a workbench or stairwell. Debris can drop from the floors above, so cover matters more than compass direction.

This one is also a reminder that many Texas homes don't have a basement at all. Many homes here are slab-on-grade, which is why the usual underground advice doesn't apply. Read more about that in [why Texas homes don't have basements](/blog/why-texas-homes-dont-have-basements/).

## Myth: Lakes, rivers, hills and city centers protect you

People sometimes believe a tornado will weaken or split around a river, climb over a hill or avoid a downtown. Tornadoes cross all of those. They can move over water, run up and down terrain, and pass through cities.

A few details are worth knowing:

- Tornadoes can cross a lake or river without breaking up. A tornado that moves from land onto water can stay a tornado.
- Hills and ridges don't create a protected zone.
- Large cities have been struck. Tall buildings don't prevent tornadoes, and a sturdy structure is no guarantee of safety without a proper shelter area inside.

If you live near Lake Texoma, the water gives you no protection, and it can leave you with fewer places to go. See our guide to [Lake Texoma tornado safety](/blog/lake-texoma-tornado-safety/).

## Myth: Tornadoes don't hit the same place twice

Every tornado is a fresh roll of the dice. The same neighborhood, town or even street can be hit more than once, and nothing about a past tornado makes a place safer. Local history is useful for understanding risk but it isn't a protection. If you're curious about the region's record, our overview of [Sherman tornado history](/blog/sherman-tx-tornado-history/) is a good place to start.

## Myth: A bathtub or mattress will save you

This one deserves some nuance, because it's partly true and partly dangerous.

Getting into a bathtub and pulling a mattress over yourself is a recognized way to add protection when you don't have a shelter. A small interior bathroom puts more walls between you and the outside, and a mattress, blankets or heavy cushions help block debris. NWS guidance for people without a shelter is to go to the lowest level, get into an interior room with no windows, and cover your head and body.

Here's where it goes wrong. People take that fallback advice and treat it as equal to a tested shelter. It isn't. A bathtub in a standard house isn't designed to survive a direct hit, and a mattress won't stop heavy debris at high speed. It's better than standing in a hallway, and a lot worse than a tested safe room built to FEMA P-320 criteria and ICC 500. Think of it as a last-ditch plan, not a long-term one. ## Myth: A tornado sounds like a freight train, so you'll hear it coming

Plenty of survivors describe a freight-train roar. That's a real description, but turning it into a warning system is a mistake.

Not every tornado makes that sound. Some are quiet, or are drowned out by rain, hail, thunder and wind. At night or inside a house with the TV on, you may hear nothing until it's very close. And if you wait to hear it, you've already used up most of your time. Rely on alerts, not your ears. A NOAA Weather Radio, Wireless Emergency Alerts and a local broadcast are far more reliable than the noise. Our article on [signs of a tornado](/blog/signs-of-a-tornado/) explains what to watch for, and why you shouldn't count on any single sign.

## Myth: If you can't see a funnel, there's no tornado

Rain-wrapped tornadoes, nighttime storms and dust or debris can hide a tornado entirely. Some tornadoes don't have a clear funnel at all, and the circulation reaches the ground through a cloud of debris. If a warning is issued for your area, act on it. Don't wait to spot a funnel from the window. For after-dark risk specifically, read about [nighttime tornadoes](/blog/nighttime-tornadoes/).

## Myth: Once the wind dies down, it's over

The danger doesn't always pass the moment the wind quiets. Other storm cells can follow, and damaged buildings, downed lines and gas leaks create new hazards. Wait for the warning to expire or for officials to say the threat has passed, and read [what to do after a tornado](/blog/after-a-tornado/) before you head outside.

## Myth: Weak tornadoes aren't a big deal

The Enhanced Fujita scale rates tornadoes after the fact, based on damage surveys, from EF0 (about 65 to 85 mph) up to EF5 (over 200 mph). A lower rating doesn't mean you can ignore a warning. Winds at the low end of the scale can still throw debris, break windows and take shingles off, and flying objects are the main hazard in any tornado. The rating also comes after the storm, so it can't help you decide whether to take cover in the moment. Our guide to the [Enhanced Fujita scale](/blog/enhanced-fujita-scale/) explains how the ratings work.

## Myth: A watch and a warning mean about the same thing

They don't. A tornado watch means conditions are right for tornadoes to form, and the Storm Prediction Center issues it. A tornado warning means a tornado is occurring or is indicated by radar, and your local NWS office issues it. A watch means get ready. A warning means take shelter now. If you need a refresher, see [tornado watch vs. warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/).

## Why the myths stick, and what replaces them

Most of these ideas survive because they're easy to remember and sound logical. The truth is less satisfying: there's no trick. The things that actually protect you are mostly boring:

1. Get the warning (radio, phone alerts, more than one source).
2. Move early instead of waiting to confirm.
3. Get to the strongest, lowest, most interior place you have.
4. Cover your head and body.
5. Stay put until the threat is over.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I open my windows during a tornado?

No. The NWS says opening windows doesn't reduce damage and wastes time you need to get to shelter. Houses are damaged by wind and debris, not pressure differences. Stay away from windows and go to your safe spot right away.

### Is an overpass a safe place to hide from a tornado?

No. An overpass can funnel wind and debris and leaves you exposed, and it blocks traffic for other people. If you're caught on the road, the guidance is to get into a sturdy building if you can reach one safely. Our driving guide covers the options in more detail.

### Does the southwest corner of the basement really matter?

Not in a way you can count on. Tornadoes can arrive from any direction, so no corner is reliably safer. Being on the lowest level, away from windows and under something sturdy is what matters. Many Texas homes have no basement, so a tested shelter is the better answer.

### Do lakes, rivers or hills stop a tornado?

No. Tornadoes cross water, climb hills and move through towns. Living near a lake or river gives you no protection, so you should treat a warning the same way wherever you are.

### Can a tornado hit the same town twice?

Yes. A past tornado doesn't make a town safer or more likely to be hit again. Each storm is independent, so keep your plan in place regardless of local history.

### Is a bathtub with a mattress over me good enough?

It's better than nothing, and it's a common fallback when you have no shelter. But it isn't a tested shelter and wasn't designed to withstand a direct hit. A safe room built to FEMA P-320 criteria and tested to ICC 500 gives far better protection.

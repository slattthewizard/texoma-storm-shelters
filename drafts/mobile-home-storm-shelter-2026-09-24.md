# Storm Shelters for Mobile Homes: Safe Options for Manufactured Homes in North Texas

A storm shelter for a mobile home has to be a structure separate from the home itself. The National Weather Service is direct about this: mobile and manufactured homes aren't safe in a tornado, tied down or not, and the fix isn't a stronger anchor, it's somewhere else to go. For rural households across Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties, that "somewhere else" usually means one of three things: an above-ground safe room on its own slab, an underground shelter close to the door, or a shared shelter serving a whole park or small community.

## Why the Home Itself Can't Be the Shelter

Tie-down systems keep a manufactured home from shifting off its foundation in ordinary high wind. They aren't rated for a tornado, and they don't do anything for the people inside if the home's structure fails. A manufactured home's frame and floor system also can't anchor a true safe room. The steel and concrete units built to FEMA P-320 and tested to ICC 500 need a foundation that can resist windborne debris and uplift on its own, which is one reason a safe room for a mobile home almost always sits on a separate slab next to the home rather than inside it or bolted to it.

This is also why NWS guidance for mobile home residents is different from guidance for a stick-built house. In a site-built home with no safe room, the advice is to get to the lowest floor and an interior room away from windows. In a mobile home, there usually isn't a lowest floor or an interior room that helps. The guidance is to leave before the storm arrives, not to ride it out. Read the full breakdown at [safest place in a tornado](/blog/safest-place-in-a-tornado/) and the difference between a [tornado watch and a warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/), because a watch is your window to get to a shelter and a warning means you're already out of time.

## Three Real Options for Manufactured Home Sites

### An Above-Ground Safe Room on Its Own Slab

This is a steel unit set on a dedicated concrete pad a short walk from the home's door, not attached to the mobile home's own structure. Because it has its own foundation, it doesn't depend on the home's floor system for anchoring, and it's the option most homeowners on a single lot end up choosing when there's room for a pad close to the house. See [above-ground safe rooms](/above-ground-safe-rooms/) for how these are built and sized.

### An Underground Shelter Near the Door

A below-grade concrete shelter, either slope-front or flat-top, is the other common choice, especially where a household wants something below the sightline of the yard. Underground shelters need a soil and drainage check before installation. Grayson County's Blackland Prairie clay holds water and shifts with wet and dry cycles, and a shelter that isn't properly anchored or ballasted can take on water or be pushed upward when the ground saturates. That's engineering to get right at install, not a reason to avoid an underground shelter, but it is a reason the site gets assessed before anyone pours concrete. Details are at [underground storm shelters](/underground-storm-shelters/).

### A Shared Shelter for a Mobile Home Park or Small Community

Where several homes sit close together, whether that's a mobile home park, a family compound, or a small cluster of manufactured homes off a county road, a single larger shelter serving all of them is often more practical than a unit at every home. This is usually a landlord or park owner's project rather than an individual resident's, and it's frequently built to FEMA P-361 community safe room criteria rather than the P-320 residential standard, because it needs to hold more people and meet a different design and governance bar. If you manage a park or a small development, [commercial storm shelters](/commercial-storm-shelters/) covers what that process looks like.

## Distance, Lighting and a Clear Path Matter as Much as the Shelter Itself

A shelter that's technically adequate but hard to reach in the dark, in bad weather, at 2 a.m., isn't much of a shelter. A few things matter more for manufactured home sites than they do for a house with an attached garage or a basement door off the kitchen:

How far it is from the door people actually use tops the list. Every extra second in the open during a warning is a second you don't get back, and closer beats farther even if it means a smaller pad. Whether the path is lit matters too. A motion light or a simple fixture on a timer near the shelter door changes how fast people move at night.

- Path is clear and stable underfoot. Gravel, mud and loose steps slow anyone down, and they're a real hazard for an older resident or someone using a cane or walker.
- Everyone in the home can get there and in, unassisted if needed. A shelter that works for a healthy adult but not for a grandparent who lives with them isn't sized or placed right for that household.

## Choosing a Spot: A Checklist

Before you settle on a slab location or dig, walk the site with this list:

- Distance from the main door is short enough to cover in under a minute on foot, not a sprint across the property
- Path is on solid, level ground (not the low spot where water collects)
- A light source reaches the path and the shelter door
- No overhead power lines or large dead limbs directly above the planned spot
- Ground doesn't pool water after rain (relevant for both underground shelter siting and general safety)
- Enough room for the shelter's footprint plus a couple of feet of clearance for the door to swing or slide fully open
- Every household member, including kids, older adults and anyone with mobility limits, can reach and enter it on their own
- If it's a shared shelter, there's a plan for who has a key or code and how neighbors know it's open during a warning

## Shared Shelters: A Landlord or Park Owner Project

A single shared shelter for a mobile home park is a bigger undertaking than a residential safe room, both in scope and in who's responsible for it. It typically falls under FEMA P-361 community safe room guidance rather than the P-320 residential standard, which affects occupant capacity planning, accessibility requirements and how the structure is tested and documented. It's usually the park owner or a small community association that initiates this kind of project, not an individual tenant. Look at the [FEMA safe rooms program](https://www.fema.gov/emergency-managers/risk-management/safe-rooms) page for how these projects are typically structured and funded before assuming it all comes out of pocket.

## What About the Grayson County Rebate?

Grayson County runs a Residential Safe Room Rebate Program that reimburses half the installed cost, up to $3,000, for an eligible single-family home outside the mapped 100-year floodplain, but you have to apply and get an award letter before installation starts. Whether a manufactured home qualifies is a question for the county's Office of Emergency Management directly, and program funding opens and closes in rounds, so check current status before you plan around it. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I put a storm shelter inside my mobile home?

No. A manufactured home's frame and floor system aren't built to anchor a safe room, and the home itself isn't safe in a tornado regardless of tie-downs. The shelter needs its own foundation, separate from the home, whether that's an above-ground unit on its own slab or an underground shelter nearby.

### How far should a storm shelter be from a mobile home's door?

As close as the site allows. The goal is to reach it in well under a minute on foot from the door people actually use, on a clear, lit, level path. A shelter that's technically nearby but across uneven ground or unlit at night effectively adds risk instead of removing it.

### Does the Grayson County storm shelter rebate cover a manufactured home?

The program is written for eligible single-family homes in Grayson County, and whether a manufactured home qualifies is a question to put directly to the county's Office of Emergency Management before you apply. Fannin and Cooke County homeowners should check with their own county.

### What's the difference between an above-ground safe room and an underground shelter for a mobile home site?

An above-ground safe room sits on its own slab at ground level, which makes it easier to access and inspect and avoids soil and drainage concerns. An underground shelter sits below grade and needs a soil and drainage assessment first, since clay soils common in this area can hold water and affect anchoring.

### Who pays for a shared storm shelter at a mobile home park?

It's typically a project the park owner or a small community association takes on, since a shared shelter serving multiple households is usually built to FEMA P-361 community safe room criteria rather than a single-household residential standard. Individual tenants aren't generally expected to fund it alone.

### What's the difference between a tornado watch and a tornado warning?

A watch means conditions favor tornadoes forming in and around the area over the next several hours, issued by the Storm Prediction Center. A warning means a tornado has actually been sighted or indicated by radar, issued by the local National Weather Service office, and it means taking shelter immediately rather than waiting to see what develops.

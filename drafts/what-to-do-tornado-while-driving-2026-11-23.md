# What to Do If You're Caught in a Tornado While Driving

A car is one of the worst places to be when a tornado is nearby. It's light, it's mostly glass, and a tornado can toss it like a toy. That's not meant to scare you. It's the reason the advice for a tornado while driving comes down to one idea: get out of the vehicle and into a sturdy building before the storm reaches you, if you possibly can.

Here in the Texoma area, plenty of us spend a lot of time on the road. People commute along US-75 through Sherman and Denison, run US-82 between Gainesville and Bonham, or cross the lake into Oklahoma. A tornado warning doesn't wait until you're home. So it's worth knowing the plan before you need it.

## Plan to avoid the situation first

The best way to handle a tornado while driving is to not be driving into it. A few habits make a big difference.

- Check the forecast before long trips on days when severe weather is possible. Our guide to [severe weather risk categories](/blog/severe-weather-risk-categories/) explains how to read the day's outlook in about a minute.
- Know the difference between a watch and a warning. A watch means conditions could produce tornadoes. A warning means one is happening or about to. If you want a refresher, see [tornado watch vs warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/).
- Keep alerts turned on. Wireless Emergency Alerts push tornado warnings to phones in the warned area with no app or sign-up, and we cover how they work in [tornado alerts on your phone](/blog/tornado-alerts-on-your-phone/).
- Delay the trip or pull off early. If a line of storms is moving toward your route, waiting an hour somewhere safe costs you far less than a bad decision at 70 mph.

Spring is the main tornado season in North Texas, roughly March through June, and there's a smaller bump in the fall. But tornadoes have occurred here in every month, so don't treat winter driving as automatically safe.

## The first choice: a sturdy building

When a tornado warning is issued and you're on the road, the National Weather Service's guidance is to take shelter in a sturdy building if you can reach one. That means a place built to hold up: a store, a school, a church, a government building, a hospital, a restaurant, a home. Go in, get to the lowest floor, and move to an interior room or hallway away from windows.

Don't wait until you can see the tornado. Pull off at the next exit, into the next parking lot, or at the first building you can safely reach. Don't be shy about asking to come in during a warning. A few minutes of awkwardness is nothing compared to what a tornado does to a car.

A few details matter here:

- Park out of the way of other traffic and emergency vehicles.
- Leave the car, even if it's raining hard. The building is the shelter, not the vehicle.
- Avoid big open-span buildings with wide roofs if there's a better choice nearby, like a gym, big-box store or warehouse. If you have no other option, get as low as possible and as far from the large open area as you can.
- Skip mobile homes and trailers. They're not safe places to wait out a tornado, and our article on [mobile home storm shelters](/blog/mobile-home-storm-shelter/) explains why.

If you're not sure where to go inside once you get there, [the safest place in a tornado](/blog/safest-place-in-a-tornado/) breaks it down room by room.

## Overpasses are not shelter

This one is worth saying clearly, because the myth has stuck around for years. Don't shelter under a highway overpass or bridge. The National Weather Service has long warned against it. The space under an overpass can act like a wind tunnel, speeding up the wind and debris as it funnels through. You also have no protection from flying objects, and you'll be stopping where you may block traffic and other people trying to escape.

Stopping under an overpass on US-75 is a bad plan even on an ordinary day. During a tornado warning, with traffic stacking up behind you, it can make things worse. If you want to see more of what people get wrong, our page on [tornado myths](/blog/tornado-myths/) covers the common ones.

## If you can see the tornado and there's no shelter nearby

Sometimes there's no building anywhere near you. Open stretches of Highway 82 and farm-to-market roads in Fannin and Cooke counties can go a long way without a sturdy structure. Here's how to think about it.

If the tornado is far away, you can clearly see it, and the roads are open and uncongested, you may be able to drive out of its path. The key is to drive at a right angle to its movement, not straight ahead or straight back along the same road. Tornadoes can change direction, and they can move fast, so this only works when it's genuinely far off and traffic isn't slowing you down.

Don't try to outrun it if the traffic is heavy, if you can't see the tornado clearly, if it's close, or if it's night or raining hard enough that you can't tell where it is. Rain-wrapped tornadoes are especially hard to spot. If you're not sure, assume it's closer than you think. For what to look and listen for, see [signs of a tornado](/blog/signs-of-a-tornado/).

## Last resort options when there's nowhere to go

If you're caught on the road, can't reach a sturdy building and can't drive away, the guidance from the National Weather Service gives you two bad options. Pick the better of the two based on what's around you.

**Option 1: Stay in the vehicle.**

1. Keep your seat belt on.
2. Put your head down below the windows.
3. Cover your head and neck with your hands, and if you have one, a blanket, coat or cushion.

**Option 2: Get out and lie low, but only if you can get noticeably lower than the road.**

If there's a ditch or low spot that's clearly below the level of the roadway, you may be able to lie flat in it and cover your head and neck with your hands. Get well clear of the car, because vehicles can be thrown or rolled. Watch out for flooding, since a ditch can fill quickly in heavy rain. If the area is flat and the low spot is shallow, you won't gain much, and staying buckled in the car may be the safer call.

Neither of these is a good plan. They're what you do when nothing better exists. The goal is to spend as little time as possible in either situation, which is why getting to a building early matters so much.

## Quick reference: what to do based on where you are

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Warning issued and a sturdy building is nearby | Leave the car and go inside to the lowest floor, interior room, away from windows |
| Tornado visible but far away, open roads, light traffic | Drive away at a right angle to its path |
| Under or near an overpass | Don't stop there and don't shelter under it |
| No building within reach, tornado close | Stay buckled in, head below the windows, cover your head, or lie in a ditch well clear of the car if it's clearly below road level |
| Heavy traffic or can't see the tornado | Don't try to outrun it; head for the nearest building |

## After the storm passes

Once the tornado has gone by, stay put for a bit. Other storms in the line may follow, and the area may have downed power lines, debris and damaged roads. If anyone is hurt, call 911. Don't drive through debris fields or standing water, and treat any downed line as live. For the next steps once the danger has passed, see our guide on [what to do after a tornado](/blog/after-a-tornado/).

## What you can do before you're on the road

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I get out of my car during a tornado?

If a sturdy building is nearby, yes. Leave the car and get inside. If there's no building within reach, the guidance is to either stay buckled in with your head below the windows and covered, or lie in a ditch that's clearly lower than the road and well clear of the car. Neither is good, so reaching a real building early is always the better plan.

### Is it safe to shelter under an overpass during a tornado?

No. The National Weather Service advises against it. An overpass can funnel wind and debris, it gives no protection from flying objects, and stopping there can block traffic. Head for a sturdy building instead.

### Can I outrun a tornado in my car?

Only in limited cases. If the tornado is far away, clearly visible, and traffic is light, you may be able to drive away at a right angle to its path. If it's close, hidden by rain, or the roads are crowded, don't try. Tornadoes can change direction and speed, and a stalled line of cars is a very dangerous place to be.

### What should I do if there's a tornado warning and I'm on the highway?

Take the next exit and look for the closest sturdy building: a store, restaurant, school, church or other solid structure. Get inside and move to the lowest floor, away from windows. Don't wait until you see the tornado, because rain and darkness can hide it.

### Is a ditch safer than staying in the car?

It can be, but only if the ditch is clearly lower than the road, and you can get well away from the vehicle. A shallow dip gives little protection, and ditches can flood in heavy rain. If you can't find a clearly lower spot, staying belted in with your head down below the windows and covered is the other option the National Weather Service describes.

### How do I get tornado warnings while I'm driving?

Wireless Emergency Alerts send warnings straight to phones in the warned area, with no app or sign-up. A NOAA Weather Radio and local radio can also help. Keep alerts on, and don't rely on seeing the storm yourself.

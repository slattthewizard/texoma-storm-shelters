# Tornado Watch vs Warning: What Each Means and What to Do in Texoma

A tornado watch means conditions are lined up for tornadoes to form somewhere in the watch area over the next several hours. A tornado warning means a tornado has actually been spotted or picked up on radar, right now, and you need to take shelter immediately. That's not a wording difference. One means get ready. The other means go.

## Who Issues Each Alert, and Why That Matters

A tornado **watch** comes from the Storm Prediction Center (SPC) in Norman, Oklahoma. SPC meteorologists look at the broader atmosphere, the instability, wind shear, moisture, and lift, and draw a watch box over a region where those ingredients are in place. A watch usually covers several counties or more, can last for hours, and doesn't mean a tornado is happening. It means the setup is right for one to happen.

A tornado **warning** comes from your local National Weather Service office. Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties are covered by NWS Fort Worth. A warning is issued when a forecaster sees actual rotation on radar or gets a confirmed report of a tornado on the ground. Warnings are small and short: usually a single county or part of one, and they typically run 30 to 45 minutes before they're extended, cancelled, or replaced.

That's the core distinction: SPC watches the atmosphere and issues watches. NWS Fort Worth watches the storm itself and issues warnings. One is forecast, the other is happening.

## Watch vs Warning at a Glance

| | Tornado Watch | Tornado Warning |
|---|---|---|
| Who issues it | Storm Prediction Center (SPC) | Local NWS office (Fort Worth for Texoma) |
| What it means | Conditions favor tornadoes forming | A tornado is confirmed or radar-indicated |
| Typical size | Multiple counties, a large region | One county or part of one |
| Typical duration | Several hours | 30 to 45 minutes, can be extended |
| What you do | Get ready, stay alert | Take shelter now |

## Texoma's Tornado Season, and Why a Watch Still Matters Off-Season

Texas tornadoes peak in spring, roughly April through June, with May typically the busiest month statewide. North Texas also sees a smaller secondary bump in the fall as cold fronts start clashing with warm Gulf air again. That doesn't mean the rest of the year is off the hook. Tornadoes have touched down in Texas in every month, so a watch in January or August deserves the same prep list as one in May. For a fuller breakdown of how the risk shifts through the year in this region, see [Texas tornado season](/blog/texas-tornado-season/).

## What to Do When a Watch Is Issued

A watch is your window to get everything in place before the pressure is on. Don't wait for the warning to start checking these off.

- Confirm your safe room or shelter door opens and closes freely and nothing is stacked in front of it
- Charge phones and any battery packs, and make sure a NOAA Weather Radio or another battery-powered radio works
- Put on real shoes, not socks or sandals, in case you're walking over broken glass or debris later
- Know where kids, elderly family members and pets are, and how fast you can gather everyone
- Move your emergency kit into or right next to the shelter if it isn't already there
- Check that your car isn't blocking a path you'd need to reach the shelter
- If you're at work, running errands or on the road, know where the nearest sturdy building is

A watch is also the time to glance at [safest place in a tornado](/blog/safest-place-in-a-tornado/) if you haven't settled on a shelter spot yet, and to review your [emergency kit](/blog/emergency-kit-storm-shelter/) while you still have a few hours instead of a few minutes.

## What to Do When a Warning Is Issued

Go now. A warning means don't wait to see the sky, don't wait to hear a siren, don't finish what you're doing first.

Get into your safe room, storm shelter or the most interior, lowest room in the house, away from windows and exterior walls, and cover your head and neck. If you're in a mobile or manufactured home, leave it. NWS guidance is clear that mobile homes aren't safe in a tornado even when tied down, so get to a sturdy building or shelter before the storm arrives, not during it. Don't try to outrun a tornado in a car, and don't shelter under a highway overpass. Cars and overpasses are both worse than staying put in a proper shelter.

If you're already in an aboveground safe room or underground shelter, stay there until the warning is cancelled or your local station confirms it's clear. Don't step out to look.

## How You Actually Get the Alert

Sirens are built for people outdoors, and they're not designed to be heard clearly inside a house, over a TV, or while you're asleep. Treat them as a backup, never your primary warning source. What actually reaches you inside the house is your phone, a dedicated radio, and whatever's on the TV.

Wireless Emergency Alerts push straight to your cell phone from the National Weather Service when a warning covers your location. No app required, and they work even if you never signed up for anything.

A NOAA Weather Radio is a dedicated receiver that stays on and sounds an alarm for watches and warnings in your county, including overnight when your phone might be on silent. Get one for the house and one for the shelter.

Local TV and radio stations in the Dallas-Fort Worth market break into programming for warnings covering Texoma and will usually show the warned area on a map, so you can judge how close the threat actually is.

For the technical difference between the two alert types straight from the source, see the [National Weather Service's watch vs warning page](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-ww).

## Tornado Emergency and PDS Watch: The Higher-End Wording

Two terms show up in bad outbreaks that go beyond a standard watch or warning.

A **PDS watch**, short for "particularly dangerous situation," is a tornado watch SPC issues when the atmosphere is set up for an unusually high chance of strong, long-lived tornadoes rather than a typical watch day. It's still a watch, meaning it's still about getting ready, but it's a signal to take the prep list above more seriously and faster.

A **tornado emergency** is a rare warning-level statement your local NWS office issues when a large, confirmed, life-threatening tornado is actively moving through a populated area. It doesn't replace the tornado warning, it escalates the language inside one. If you ever hear it, you're already past the "watch and see" stage. Get to shelter immediately.

## A Warning Only Helps If You Have Somewhere to Go

None of this matters much if the warning goes out and there's nowhere in your house built to take a direct hit. A closet or bathroom helps against flying glass and falling debris, but it isn't built to the wind and debris-impact standards a purpose-built shelter is. Storm shelters and safe rooms in this region are built to withstand the 250 mph design wind speed that all of North Texas falls under, and a shelter built to FEMA P-320 or P-361 criteria and tested to ICC 500 is engineered specifically for that.

Most Texoma homes don't have a basement to fall back on. Expansive clay soils that shrink and swell, shallow limestone in places, and the cost of waterproofing against a high water table have kept slab-on-grade construction standard across Grayson County for decades, so basements are the exception, not the norm.

That leaves two practical paths for most homeowners: an [above-ground safe room](/above-ground-safe-rooms/) anchored inside or attached to the house, or a [garage floor shelter](/garage-floor-shelters/) that stays out of the way until you need it. Both put shelter exactly where a warning gives you the least time to reach it. If you'd rather go underground, know that clay soils can hold water against a buried shelter, and anchoring and drainage matter more here than in most parts of the country. A proper site assessment should catch that before anything gets dug.

If cost is what's holding you back, the [storm shelter cost guide](/storm-shelter-cost/) breaks down real ranges by shelter type. Grayson County homeowners should also look at the [county's rebate program](/blog/grayson-county-storm-shelter-rebate/) before they install anything, since the application has to happen before the work starts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a tornado watch and a tornado warning?

A watch means conditions favor tornadoes forming somewhere in a broad area over the next several hours, issued by the Storm Prediction Center. A warning means a tornado has been spotted or shown on radar in your specific area right now, issued by your local NWS office, and it means take shelter immediately.

### How long does a tornado warning usually last in Texoma?

Most tornado warnings run 30 to 45 minutes before National Weather Service Fort Worth extends, replaces, or cancels them based on what the storm is doing. Stay in shelter until the warning is officially lifted rather than guessing based on how the weather looks outside.

### Should I rely on outdoor sirens to know when to take shelter?

No. Sirens are meant to alert people who are outside and aren't reliable indoors, overnight, or over background noise. Wireless Emergency Alerts on your phone and a NOAA Weather Radio are both built to reach you inside your home and should be your primary alerts.

### What does a severe thunderstorm warning mean compared to a tornado warning?

A severe thunderstorm warning covers damaging wind and large hail from a storm that isn't currently producing a tornado. A tornado warning is issued specifically when a tornado is confirmed or radar-indicated. Severe thunderstorms can and do produce tornadoes with little notice, which is part of why having a shelter ready matters even on a severe thunderstorm day.

### Is a closet or bathroom good enough during a tornado warning?

An interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows, is the best option if you have no other choice, and it does reduce risk from flying glass and some debris. It isn't built to the wind speed and debris-impact standards a purpose-built safe room or storm shelter meets, so it's a fallback, not a substitute for one.

### What should I do if I'm in a mobile home when a warning is issued?

Leave. National Weather Service guidance is that mobile and manufactured homes aren't safe in a tornado even when tied down. Get to a sturdy building or a storm shelter before severe weather arrives in your area, since a warning doesn't give you time to relocate once it's issued.

# Lake Texoma Tornado Safety: What Boaters, Campers and RV Visitors Need to Know

Lake Texoma is one of the best places in the region to spend a weekend. It's also a place where a tornado warning catches people in about the worst spots possible: on open water, in a tent, in an RV or in a lightweight cabin, often miles from a sturdy building.

The lake straddles the Texas and Oklahoma line, so storms can come from either side, and the warnings for each side come from different forecast offices. This guide covers what to do before you go, how to handle a watch day, what to ask when you check in, and how to stay informed across the state line.

## Why the lake is a hard place to be in a tornado

The risks stack up in ways they don't at home.

**There's no shelter on open water.** A boat, even a large one, gives you no protection from tornadic winds, hail or lightning. There's nothing to hide behind and nowhere to go, and a tornado that moves from land onto the lake doesn't weaken just because it's over water.

**Water doesn't protect you.** It's a myth that lakes and rivers stop tornadoes. You can read more of these in our guide to [tornado myths](/blog/tornado-myths/).

**You're far from a sturdy building.** Even a short run to shore can take longer than you think once you factor in distance, wind, chop and the time it takes to secure the boat.

**Tents and RVs offer no protection.** A tent is fabric on poles. An RV or travel trailer is a thin shell that can roll, flip or break apart in strong winds, long before a tornado arrives. A pop-up camper, a cabin of thin wood or a manufactured unit isn't much better. None of these are designed for tornadoes.

**Warnings are harder to receive.** Cell coverage may be patchy on the water and in some coves, and sirens may not reach every cove or campsite. People on the water may not hear or see a warning at all.

**You're often unfamiliar with the area.** Visitors don't know the nearest sturdy building, the roads, or who runs the local alerts. That's the biggest reason for planning ahead.

## Before you go: check the forecast like it's part of your packing list

Look at the forecast for the day, not just the hour. The Storm Prediction Center's convective outlook uses five categories: Marginal, Slight, Enhanced, Moderate and High. If your trip falls on an Enhanced day or higher, or if a tornado watch is in effect, seriously consider changing plans. Our guide to [severe weather risk categories](/blog/severe-weather-risk-categories/) explains what each level means.

Here's the practical version:

- **Marginal or Slight:** Go, but stay alert and keep a plan in your head.
- **Enhanced or above:** Think about postponing, or choose activities near sturdy shelter rather than on open water or at an exposed campsite.
- **Watch issued:** Stay close to a sturdy building, keep the boat at the dock, and monitor alerts. A watch means conditions are right for tornadoes to form. See [tornado watch vs. warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/) for the difference.
- **Warning issued:** Take shelter immediately. A warning means a tornado is occurring or indicated by radar.

Spring is the main peak across North Texas, roughly March through June, with a smaller bump in fall. But tornadoes can happen in any month, so don't assume a winter or summer weekend is automatically safe. Our overview of [Texas tornado season](/blog/texas-tornado-season/) has more.

## Boaters: get off the lake early

Boating is where timing matters most, because you can't outrun a fast-moving storm in open water and you can't take shelter on a boat.

1. **Decide your cutoff before you leave the dock.** If storms are possible, plan to be back well before they're expected, not after the first rumble.
2. **Don't wait for the warning.** On a watch day, or when you can see towering clouds building or the sky darkening, head in early. By the time a warning arrives, you may not have enough time to reach shore.
3. **Know where you'd land.** Look at the shoreline and think about where you can tie up and walk to a sturdy building. Don't plan on sheltering in a boat house, pavilion or open-air structure.
4. **Tell someone your plan.** Let someone on shore know where you're going and when you expect to be back.
5. **Keep a way to get alerts on board.** A handheld NOAA Weather Radio or a marine radio that receives weather alerts is a good backup when phone service drops out.
6. **Wear a life jacket.** Winds can capsize a boat quickly, and rough water is dangerous even in a lightweight storm.

If a warning is issued while you're on the water, head for the closest shore, tie up or beach the boat, and get into the sturdiest building you can reach on foot. Don't stay with the boat. If there's truly no building nearby, find the lowest ground you can, away from trees, and cover your head and body. That's a last resort, not a plan.

## Campers: ask about shelter when you check in

Campgrounds vary a lot. Some have a sturdy building on site, and some don't. The simplest way to avoid guessing is to ask at check-in, before the weather's a problem.

Ask these questions of the staff or host:

- Where's the nearest sturdy building I can get to on foot?
- Is it open to campers at night, and who unlocks it?
- How does the campground warn people about severe weather? Is there a siren, a text system or a PA?
- Where should I go if I'm in a tent, an RV or a cabin?
- What's the plan if a warning comes in the middle of the night?

Write down the answers, or put the location in your phone. If there's no good answer, treat that as information. It's better to know before dark that your plan is to drive to a nearby town than to find out at 2 a.m. Our article on [nighttime tornadoes](/blog/nighttime-tornadoes/) explains why overnight warnings are especially risky for campers.

**Practical steps for campers:**

- Pick a campsite with a clear path to a road.
- Keep your car keys, shoes, flashlight and phone within reach at night.
- On a watch day, be ready to pack up fast.
- Don't shelter in a tent or in your vehicle if you can reach a building.

## RV and cabin visitors: lightweight isn't safe

Many visitors assume a cabin or RV is a "real" building. In a tornado, it isn't a shelter.

**RVs and travel trailers.** They can be moved, rolled or destroyed by strong winds, and even a severe thunderstorm can do serious damage. If a warning is issued, leave the RV and go to the nearest sturdy building. If there's no building, your options are limited, and that's why you should find out ahead of time.

**Cabins and rentals.** Some are well built, but few are designed as shelters, and many lack a basement or an interior room with no windows. Ask the owner or manager whether there's a safe space, and if there isn't, know the plan for getting to one. If you're staying in a rental, look for an interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows, such as a closet or bathroom, and use pillows, blankets or mattresses to cover yourself. That's a fallback, not a substitute for a tested shelter.

**Mobile and manufactured homes.** A mobile or manufactured home isn't a safe place to ride out a tornado either. If you live in one near the lake, our guide to [mobile home storm shelter options](/blog/mobile-home-storm-shelter/) covers what you can add.

## Staying informed on both sides of the lake

Lake Texoma sits across a state line, and that affects which forecast office is talking to you.

- **Texas side (Grayson, Fannin, Cooke counties):** warnings come from the NWS Fort Worth office (NWS Dallas/Fort Worth).
- **Oklahoma side (Bryan, Marshall, Love counties):** warnings come from NWS Norman.

A tornado on one side can cross over to the other, and if you're on the water you might be near either county. That's why a NOAA Weather Radio programmed with more than one SAME county code is useful. The codes are:

| County | SAME code |
|---|---|
| Grayson, TX | 048181 |
| Fannin, TX | 048147 |
| Cooke, TX | 048097 |
| Bryan, OK | 040013 |
| Marshall, OK | 040095 |
| Love, OK | 040085 |

Enter the counties around where you'll be, and you'll get alerts for whichever side the storm is threatening. Our guide to [NOAA Weather Radio in the Texoma area](/blog/noaa-weather-radio-texoma/) goes through setup.

Wireless Emergency Alerts also push warnings to phones in the warned area with no app or sign-up, so keep your phone charged and make sure emergency alerts are turned on. They're a good layer but not a guarantee, especially if you're in a spot with weak service. Read more in [tornado alerts on your phone](/blog/tornado-alerts-on-your-phone/), and if you want to follow the storm yourself, [how to read tornado radar](/blog/how-to-read-tornado-radar/) is a useful primer.

## If a warning is issued and you're not near a shelter

Sometimes you just can't get to a sturdy building in time. Here's the short list of what to do when options are limited:

1. Get off the water immediately and away from the boat.
2. Move to the most substantial building you can reach on foot, such as a permanent concrete or block structure, and get to the lowest floor, in an interior room away from windows.
3. If you're in a vehicle and a building isn't reachable, our guide on [what to do in a tornado while driving](/blog/what-to-do-tornado-while-driving/) lays out the options.
4. If you're caught outside with no cover, get as low as you can and protect your head and neck.

None of these is a good option. All of them are far worse than being somewhere safe before the storm arrives, which is the whole point of planning ahead.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is there anywhere safe to shelter on Lake Texoma if a tornado is coming?

Not on the water. A boat gives you no protection, and tents, RVs and light cabins aren't safe either. Your best option is a sturdy permanent building on shore. Find out where it is before you need it, and get off the lake early on any day with a watch or a high risk level.

### What should I ask a campground when I check in?

Ask where the nearest sturdy building is, whether it's open to campers at night, how the campground warns people about severe weather, and where to go if you're in a tent, RV or cabin. Write down the answers, since nobody wants to figure this out at midnight during a warning.

### Are RVs safe in a tornado?

No. RVs and travel trailers can be rolled or destroyed by strong winds, and they aren't designed to protect you from a tornado. If a warning is issued, leave the RV and go to the nearest sturdy building. Check where that is when you arrive.

### Which weather office covers Lake Texoma?

It depends on the side. NWS Fort Worth (NWS Dallas/Fort Worth) covers Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties in Texas. NWS Norman covers Bryan, Marshall and Love counties in Oklahoma. A NOAA Weather Radio programmed with county codes from both sides keeps you covered wherever you are on the lake.

### Should I go boating on a tornado watch day?

It's safest not to. A watch means conditions are right for tornadoes to form, and you can't shelter on a boat or outrun a fast-moving storm in open water. If you do go, stay close to shore, keep alerts on and head in at the first sign of trouble rather than waiting for a warning.

### Can I add a storm shelter at a lake property?

Often, yes. Options include above-ground safe rooms, garage floor units and underground shelters, depending on the lot, the soil and the water table. A local installer can look at your site and tell you what's workable, and your city or county building department can confirm permit requirements.

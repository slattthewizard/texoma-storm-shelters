# Storm Shelter Supplies: Lighting, Seating and What to Keep Inside Your Shelter

Once a shelter is installed, the next question is what actually goes inside it. Storm shelter supplies aren't the same as your grab-and-go emergency kit, the bag you'd bring to a hotel or a relative's house. These are the items that live in the shelter itself, ready the moment you and your family step inside. Get the basics right and a stressful few minutes underground or in a steel box gets a lot more manageable.

## Shelter supplies vs. your emergency kit

If you've already read our guide to [building an emergency kit for a storm shelter](/blog/emergency-kit-storm-shelter/), you know that kit is meant to be portable: food, medications, documents, a change of clothes. This article covers something different. These are fixtures and small items that stay in the shelter year-round, so you're not scrambling to gather them while a warning is active.

Some overlap is fine. A flashlight can live in both places. But don't treat this list as a substitute for that one, and don't treat that one as a substitute for this.

## Lighting: don't count on house power

Power is often the first thing to go in a severe storm, and an underground or garage-floor shelter can be pitch dark without it. Battery lanterns and headlamps are the standard fix. A lantern gives you hands-free light for the whole space; headlamps let each person see without holding anything, which matters if you're comforting a kid or checking on a pet.

Keep spare batteries in the shelter, not just in the lantern. Rotate them on the same schedule as your smoke detector batteries so you're not finding dead cells the one time you need them.

One rule that isn't optional: never run a generator or a fuel-burning heater inside a storm shelter, even briefly, even with the door open. Shelters are small, often sealed tighter than a house room, and carbon monoxide builds up fast in that kind of space. If you need backup power or heat after a storm, that's a job for the house, not the shelter. Our guide on [staying comfortable during a power outage](/blog/power-outage-shelter/) covers safer ways to handle that.

## Seating that doesn't block the door or the vents

Benches make sense in a shelter you might occupy for 20 minutes or more, especially with kids, older family members, or anyone who has trouble standing that long. Placement is what trips people up. A bench or folding chair set in the wrong spot can block the door swing, so the door can't fully open or close, or it can cover a vent opening and cut off the airflow the shelter depends on.

Before you add seating, check how the door swings (in or out) and where the vents sit, then plan seating around both. If you're not sure what your model allows, our [storm shelter comfort guide](/blog/storm-shelter-comfort/) walks through options that work in a range of shelter sizes, and our [guide to shelter doors](/blog/storm-shelter-doors/) explains what to expect from the door assembly itself.

## A NOAA Weather Radio, with charged batteries

A phone is fine until the signal drops or the battery dies, and storms are usually when both happen. A battery-powered NOAA Weather Radio picks up alerts straight from the National Weather Service regardless of cell coverage. Keep one in the shelter itself, not the house.

Know the difference between the two alerts you'll hear. A tornado **watch** means conditions favor tornadoes forming somewhere in a fairly large area, issued by the Storm Prediction Center, often hours ahead of time. A tornado **warning** means a tornado has actually been spotted or shown up on radar, issued by the National Weather Service office covering your county. Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties are served by NWS Fort Worth. A warning means take shelter now, not wait and see. Our breakdown of [watch versus warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/) goes deeper if you want the full picture, and the [National Weather Service's own explainer](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-ww) is worth bookmarking too.

## A way to signal, and a way to be found

If your shelter is buried or built into a hillside, sound doesn't carry the way it does above ground. A whistle or a small air horn kept inside means you can signal for help without shouting yourself hoarse if you're ever stuck waiting on someone to open the door from outside.

The other half of this is making sure people know where your shelter is in the first place. Registering your shelter, where your city or county offers it, lets emergency crews find it faster after a storm, which matters if your house is damaged and the shelter isn't visible from the street. We cover how in [register your storm shelter](/blog/register-your-storm-shelter/).

## Helmets for everyone, not just the kids

Bike or sport helmets are a common, low-cost addition for both kids and adults. The idea is simple: a helmet reduces the chance of a head injury from anything that might come loose or get tossed around near the shelter opening during a violent storm. It's general safety advice, not a requirement tied to any product claim, but it's cheap insurance and takes up almost no space on a shelf.

## A pry bar or hand tool, if your shelter calls for it

Some shelter designs include a secondary release or a spot where a pry bar helps if the primary door mechanism jams, usually from debris pressing against it outside. Whether that applies to your unit depends on the design, so ask your installer what tool, if any, they recommend keeping inside. This is general guidance, not a claim that every shelter needs one.

## Keep the vents clear, always

Vents are what keep air moving while the door is shut, and it doesn't take much to block one: a stack of storage bins, a rolled-up rug, even seating pushed too close to the wall. Walk the inside of your shelter every so often and make sure nothing has crept in front of a vent opening. Our [ventilation guide](/blog/storm-shelter-ventilation/) explains how the venting is designed to work and what to watch for.

## A small first aid kit

Cuts and scrapes happen, especially if anyone is moving quickly to get inside. A basic kit, bandages, antiseptic wipes, gauze, tape, is enough for a shelter. Save the bigger medical supplies and medications for the portable emergency kit, since those need to travel with you if you evacuate afterward.

## Water

Keep a case of bottled water or sealed water pouches in the shelter and swap it out once or twice a year so it doesn't sit past a reasonable shelf life. You may only be in there twenty minutes, but if a storm knocks out power or roads for longer, having water already on hand beats needing to go looking for it.

## Pet carriers, if pets shelter with you

A carrier or leash kept in the shelter saves time you don't have when a warning is active and a scared animal is harder to move than usual. Our [pet safety in a storm shelter guide](/blog/pet-safety-storm-shelter/) covers sizing a shelter for pets and what else to plan for.

## What NOT to store in a storm shelter

A shelter is not extra garage storage, and treating it that way creates real risk.

- Fuel of any kind: gas cans, propane tanks, kerosene. None of it belongs in an enclosed space you might need to seal up and sit in.
- **Chemicals.** Paint, solvents, pesticides and cleaning chemicals can off-gas in a small sealed room, and some are flammable.
- Clutter in front of the door: boxes, bikes, lawn equipment. Anything stacked near the entrance slows you down when seconds matter, and it can jam the door from opening fully.

If it's not on the supplies list above, it probably belongs somewhere else in the house or garage.

## Seasonal check routine

Twice a year, at the start of spring and again at the start of fall, walk through the shelter and check everything on your list: swap batteries in the lantern, headlamps and weather radio, confirm the water hasn't expired, make sure the first aid kit is stocked, and clear anything that's drifted in front of a vent or the door swing. Spring is Texas's busiest tornado stretch, roughly April through June with May typically the peak, and North Texas also sees a smaller secondary season in the fall, so checking right before each one keeps you from finding a dead battery mid-warning. Tornadoes can happen in any month, so don't skip the check just because it's an off season.

## Storm shelter supplies checklist

| Item | Why | Where to keep it |
|---|---|---|
| Battery lanterns and headlamps | Power often fails before or during the storm | Mounted or shelved, not on the floor |
| Spare batteries | Lanterns and radios run down with age even unused | Sealed container inside the shelter |
| NOAA Weather Radio | Picks up alerts when cell service doesn't | Fixed spot, easy to reach from the seat |
| Bench or seating | Comfort for longer stays, especially with kids or older family | Clear of the door swing and every vent |
| Whistle or air horn | Signals for help if sound doesn't carry outside | Hook near the door |
| Helmets | General head protection for kids and adults | Hooks or a shelf, one per family member |
| Pry bar or hand tool (if your design calls for one) | Backup if the door mechanism jams | Wherever your installer recommends |
| Small first aid kit | Basic cuts and scrapes | Shelf or wall pouch |
| Bottled water | Covers you if a stay runs longer than expected | Sealed case, rotated yearly |
| Pet carrier or leash | Saves time getting a scared pet inside | Near the door |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What lighting should I keep in a storm shelter?

Battery-powered lanterns and headlamps, plus spare batteries stored separately from the devices. Don't rely on house power or a single flashlight, since power is often one of the first things a storm takes out.

### Can I run a generator or propane heater inside a storm shelter?

No. Shelters are small and often sealed tighter than a room in your house, so carbon monoxide from a generator or fuel-burning heater can build up quickly. Keep fuel-burning equipment outside the shelter entirely, even for a short time.

### Do I still need a weather radio if I have a smartphone?

A phone is useful, but cell service and battery life aren't guaranteed during a storm. A battery-powered NOAA Weather Radio picks up National Weather Service alerts directly, so keep one in the shelter as a backup that doesn't depend on a network.

### Should benches or seating go against the vents?

No. Seating should be placed so it doesn't block the door's swing path or cover any vent opening. Check both before deciding where furniture goes, since blocking a vent cuts off the airflow the shelter is designed around.

### Can I store fuel, propane or chemicals in my shelter?

No. Fuel, propane, paint, solvents and pesticides don't belong in an enclosed space you may need to seal up and occupy. Keep those items in the garage or a separate outdoor storage area instead.

### How often should I check my shelter's supplies?

Twice a year works well: once at the start of spring and once at the start of fall, ahead of North Texas's two tornado seasons. Check batteries, water, the first aid kit and make sure nothing is blocking the door or vents.

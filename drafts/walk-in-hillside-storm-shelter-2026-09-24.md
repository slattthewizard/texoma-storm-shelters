# Walk-In and Hillside Storm Shelters: No-Stairs Options for North Texas Homes

A walk in storm shelter is any safe room or underground shelter you can enter without climbing down a ladder or a steep set of stairs. For North Texas homeowners dealing with bad knees, a wheelchair, a walker, or just a sloped lot, that single design choice outweighs the shelter's shape or material. The main routes to a no-stairs shelter are an above-ground steel safe room with a level threshold, a hillside or bermed shelter with its door built into a slope, and a ramped entry added to either one. Which one fits depends on how your lot sits, how fast everyone in the house can move, and how close the shelter is to the door you'll actually use during a warning.

## The Trouble With Stairs

Most people picture a storm shelter as a hole in the ground with a ladder or a steep flight of steps down. That works fine for someone who can move quickly and steadily. It does not work well for an older adult with a bad hip, a person using a wheelchair or a walker, a parent carrying a baby and pushing a stroller, or a household with a big dog that won't go down steps on command.

The stakes are timing, not comfort. A tornado warning means a tornado has been sighted or indicated by radar, and the National Weather Service's guidance is to get to a safe room or shelter immediately. A watch, issued earlier by the Storm Prediction Center, just means conditions favor tornadoes across a wider area for the next several hours. By the time a warning is issued for Grayson, Fannin, or Cooke County, you may have minutes. A household that moves slowly needs a shelter that's fast to reach and fast to enter, not one that adds a set of narrow, steep, possibly slick concrete steps to the process.

This is also why mobile and manufactured homes are never a safe place to ride out a tornado, even when tied down. If anyone in the house has limited mobility, the plan can't be "get everyone to the shelter eventually." It has to be "get everyone inside within a minute or two," which pushes the design question toward level entries, wide doors, and short distances.

## The Three Ways to Get a No-Stairs Shelter

### Above-Ground Safe Room With a Level Entry

An [above-ground safe room](/above-ground-safe-rooms/) is built at grade, usually as a standalone steel structure in the garage, attached to the house, or set just outside it on a slab. Because the floor of the unit sits at or near the same height as the surrounding slab or driveway, the doorway can be built with a standard-height door and little to no threshold to step over. This is generally the simplest way to get a true walk-in shelter on a flat lot, since there's no grading or excavation involved and the entry height is controllable from the start.

### Hillside or Bermed Shelter Built Into a Slope

If your lot has a natural slope, a hillside (also called a bermed or side-hill) shelter can be set into the grade so its front door sits at ground level while the rest of the structure is buried or covered with earth on the other three sides. Walk up to it the way you'd walk into a walk-out basement, no stairs required. The tradeoff is that the "uphill" side and the roof are carrying soil load and shedding runoff, so drainage and waterproofing at the door and the uphill wall become the design problem instead of the entry itself. Compare this option against a fully [underground storm shelter](/underground-storm-shelters/) before deciding, since the accessibility benefit of a hillside unit only pays off if your yard actually has usable grade to build into.

### Ramped Entries

A ramp can be added to either an above-ground unit or a hillside shelter where there's a small step or elevation change that can't be avoided, such as a threshold at a garage slab or a landing outside a bermed door. A ramp is not a substitute for planning the entry height correctly from the start; it's a fix for the inches you couldn't design away. Keep the run as long and the slope as gentle as the space allows, and use a non-slip surface, since a wet ramp during a storm approach is its own hazard.

## Door Width, Turning Space, and Other Accessibility Details

For a shelter to work for a wheelchair, a walker, or two people moving together, the door opening needs to be wider than a typical closet door, and there needs to be enough clear floor space just inside the door to turn and get out of the doorway before the next person comes in. Exact clearance numbers vary by manufacturer and by how the shelter is framed, so ask your installer to walk through the specific door and interior dimensions for any unit you're considering rather than assuming a standard size will work.

One clarification worth having up front: the formal accessibility numbers under the ADA apply to shelters built for public or community use, like a school or a government building, not to a private residential safe room. That doesn't mean accessibility doesn't matter for your home, only that a residential installer isn't required to hit a specific published number the way a [commercial storm shelter](/commercial-storm-shelters/) project would be. Ask directly what door width, threshold height, and interior turning space the unit you're looking at actually offers.

## Drainage and the Uphill Side: What Clay Soil Does to the Build

Much of Grayson County sits on Blackland Prairie clay, which shrinks and swells with moisture and is a big part of why basements are rare in Texas homes to begin with. That same clay is what makes a hillside shelter's drainage plan so important. Water that runs downhill has to be directed around the structure and away from the door, not into the uphill wall where it can pool against the shell.

Saturated clay can also push up on a buried shelter that isn't properly anchored or ballasted, a buoyancy problem that hits fiberglass and other lightweight shells hardest. A qualified installer checks your soil and grading before recommending a hillside unit, and may steer you toward an above-ground safe room instead if your lot doesn't drain well. Get that judgment call in writing before a shovel goes in the ground. For a hillside build, a real site visit and a written estimate aren't optional.

## Lighting, Handrails, and Distance From the House

A few details separate a walk-in shelter that actually gets used quickly from one that looks good on paper:

- Lighting at the entry: a motion light or a simple fixture at the door means nobody's fumbling for a phone flashlight in the dark and rain while a warning is active.
- **Handrails at any grade change.** Even a well-designed ramp or a short landing benefits from a handrail, especially for anyone unsteady on their feet.
- **A path that's usable in bad weather.** Gravel, mulch, or bare dirt between the house and the shelter can turn to mud. A firm, even surface (concrete, pavers, or compacted stone) matters as much as the shelter itself.
- Distance from the door you'll actually use: the shelter that's technically closest as the crow flies isn't much help if it means crossing the whole yard from the front door. Site it near the exit your household uses most, ideally the one closest to bedrooms if the warning comes at night.

For slow movers, closer and easier beats bigger and fancier every time. A shelter that's thirty extra feet away, or that requires walking around the house, adds real seconds for someone who can't hurry.

## Don't Forget the Rebate Question

Grayson County runs a Residential Safe Room Rebate Program, funded through FEMA and the Texas Division of Emergency Management, that reimburses 50% of the installed cost up to $3,000 for an eligible single-family home, above-ground or hillside, as long as you apply and get approval before installation and the shelter meets FEMA and ICC 500 criteria. It doesn't apply in Fannin or Cooke County, and funding rounds open and close, so check current status with the county first. Our [Grayson County storm shelter rebate](/blog/grayson-county-storm-shelter-rebate/) post walks through the process, and the county's own page is the [Grayson County OEM safe room program](https://www.co.grayson.tx.us/page/oem.saferoom).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What makes a storm shelter a "walk-in" shelter instead of a regular underground one?

A walk-in shelter has a level or near-level entry, meaning you step through the door without climbing down a ladder or a flight of stairs. That can be an above-ground safe room built at grade, or an underground or bermed shelter set into a slope so the door sits at ground level on one side even though the rest of the structure is below grade or covered with earth.

### Is a hillside storm shelter safe to build in Grayson County's clay soil?

Yes, when it's designed for the site. The key is drainage and anchoring: water has to be directed away from the door and the uphill wall, and the shelter needs to be properly anchored or ballasted so it doesn't shift in saturated clay. A qualified installer will assess your soil and grading before recommending a hillside build over an above-ground alternative.

### Can a walk-in storm shelter be made wheelchair accessible?

Generally yes, with the right door width and enough clear floor space inside to turn around, but exact dimensions vary by unit and manufacturer. Ask your installer for the specific door and interior clearances on any model you're considering rather than assuming a standard unit will fit a wheelchair or walker.

### Does the ADA require a certain door width for a home storm shelter?

The ADA's published accessibility numbers apply to shelters built for public or community use, not to a private residential safe room. That doesn't make accessibility unimportant at home, it just means there's no single required number for a homeowner's shelter, so it's worth discussing your household's specific needs directly with your installer.

### Does the Grayson County rebate cover a hillside or walk-in shelter?

It can, as long as the shelter meets FEMA and ICC 500 criteria and the home qualifies (outside the mapped floodplain, application and award letter approved before installation). The rebate reimburses 50% of the installed cost up to $3,000 and applies to Grayson County homes only, not Fannin or Cooke County.

### How close to the house should a walk-in shelter be?

As close as your layout allows to the exterior door your household actually uses most, ideally near bedrooms for a shelter that might be needed at night. For anyone who moves slowly, those extra seconds from a shelter set far across the yard hit harder than they would for someone who can jog there in ten seconds.

# DIY Storm Shelter: What FEMA P-320 Plans Require and Where Home Builds Fail

FEMA does publish storm shelter plans, and yes, they're free. FEMA P-320, "Taking Shelter from the Storm," has prescriptive construction plans for reinforced concrete, masonry, and wood-frame safe rooms with steel sheathing. The plans are real, and the engineering behind them is solid. They're written for a qualified builder though, someone who can read structural drawings, source the specified materials, and get every connection right. That's a different project than a weekend DIY build.

## What FEMA P-320 Plans Actually Cover

P-320 lays out several construction types for a residential safe room, each rated to resist the 250 mph design wind speed that applies across all of North Texas, including Grayson, Fannin, and Cooke counties. The options include:

- Reinforced concrete walls and roof, cast in place
- Concrete masonry unit (CMU) walls, fully grouted and reinforced, with a concrete roof
- Wood-frame walls sheathed with steel plate on the interior face, built to specific stud spacing and fastener schedules

Each plan set specifies rebar size and spacing, concrete strength and anchor bolt pattern, and calls for a door assembly tested to ICC 500. None of that is optional or approximate. Change one variable, thinner steel, wider stud spacing, a substitute door, and you no longer have a P-320 shelter. You have a room that looks like one.

The full standard behind these plans is ICC 500, which sets the actual test criteria. Our [FEMA P-320 standards guide](/blog/fema-p-320-storm-shelter-standards/) and our [ICC 500 requirements breakdown](/blog/icc-500-safe-room-requirements/) go deeper into both documents if you want the source material before you decide anything.

## What the Plans Demand From a Builder

### Foundation and anchorage

A safe room has to stay put through wind pressures that will try to slide it, overturn it, or lift it off its footing. P-320 specifies a foundation and anchorage system sized to resist those loads, not a generic slab. Anchor bolts, embedment depth, and slab thickness are all called out in the plans and have to match the soil and structure they're tied into. Get the anchorage wrong and the strongest walls in the world won't help, because the whole shelter can shift or separate from its foundation.

### The connections matter as much as the big pieces

Under wind load, structures tend to fail at their connections: a weld, a fastener schedule, a joint where two panels meet. That's why P-320 specifies connection details down to fastener type and spacing. A DIY builder working from a general sense of "make it strong," instead of the specified connection detail, ends up building a different, untested structure.

### A tested door assembly

This is the one people underestimate most. ICC 500 testing treats the door as a complete assembly, meaning the door panel, frame, hinges, and latching hardware are all tested together, not separately. The test itself fires a 15 lb piece of 2x4 lumber at 100 mph into the door (and the walls) to simulate wind-borne debris, and the assembly also has to hold against the wind pressure itself. A door that passed that test is only as good as its installation. Our [storm shelter doors guide](/blog/storm-shelter-doors/) covers what a tested assembly actually needs to look like.

### Debris-resistant walls and roof

Walls are tested the same way as doors, a 15 lb 2x4 at 100 mph, fired horizontally. The roof gets the same missile at 67 mph, fired vertically, since debris falling or blowing onto a roof behaves differently than debris hitting a wall. Plywood alone does not pass this test. Steel sheathing, reinforced concrete, or fully grouted masonry can, but only built to the specified thickness and reinforcement.

## Where Home Builds Usually Fail

Most DIY shelter problems trace back to one of a handful of shortcuts. None of them are obvious from the outside once the shelter is finished and painted.

The door is the one people get wrong most. A solid-core exterior door, even a heavy one, was never tested as a debris-resistant assembly, and neither was a door built at home from plywood and lumber. Without that test, there's no way to know it holds under a 100 mph impact. Walls run into a version of the same problem: a shelter can have the right wall material and still fail if it sits on a standard house slab that was never designed to resist uplift and overturning loads, or if the anchor bolts were an afterthought instead of part of the original pour.

Other shortcuts show up just as often:

- Plywood-only walls. Fine for a garage or shed, but it has no debris-impact rating on its own and isn't a P-320 wall assembly by itself.
- No ventilation. A shelter meant to hold a family for an extended stay needs air exchange. Skipping it won't hurt on a five-minute stay, but it's a real problem if a storm knocks out power or blocks the door with debris and you're stuck in there longer than planned.
- Digging in clay without shoring or drainage. Much of Grayson County sits on Blackland Prairie clay, expansive soil that shrinks and swells with moisture. An unshored excavation can collapse during construction, and once a shelter's in the ground, standing water and saturated clay can push a lightweight shell upward through buoyancy if it isn't anchored or ballasted right. Water intrusion is its own separate headache. This is why a professional install starts with a soil and drainage assessment, not a shovel.

Permit rules are their own trap. They differ from city to city and between city limits and unincorporated county land, and skipping that step can mean a shelter that's hard to document later, for a rebate, an insurance claim or a home sale. See our [Texas storm shelter permits guide](/blog/storm-shelter-permits-texas/) for what's typically required.

## The Practical Consequences

A shelter that skips any of the above isn't only a construction risk. It has real downstream costs.

Grayson County runs a Residential Safe Room Rebate Program that can reimburse up to half an installed shelter's cost, capped at $3,000, but you have to apply and get county approval before construction starts, and a shelter built without engineered plans, a permit or a tested door assembly is unlikely to pass that review. Our [Grayson County rebate guide](/blog/grayson-county-storm-shelter-rebate/) walks through the process; Fannin and Cooke County homeowners should ask their own county emergency management office whether anything similar is running.

Permitting matters on its own too, separate from any rebate. Skip it and you can run into trouble later, not just at inspection but at resale. And a shelter that can't be documented as meeting FEMA/ICC 500 criteria, no permit, no engineered plans, no record of the door assembly, may not add the value a buyer expects from a "storm shelter," and it can raise questions during a home inspection. Our [storm shelter and home value guide](/blog/storm-shelter-home-value/) covers what actually tends to matter to buyers.

## Middle-Ground Options

You don't have to choose between a full DIY build and a full-service install for everything. Two middle-ground options come up often:

1. **Handle some of the work yourself, but have a professional take on the slab, anchorage, and door.** These are the three places where a small error has the biggest consequences, and where the gap between "looks right" and "tested right" is hardest to see on your own.
2. **Buy a manufactured shelter that's already been tested as a complete unit.** A prefab steel or concrete shelter comes with documentation tying it to an actual ICC 500 test report, which is exactly what a rebate application or a future buyer will want to see. Our [prefab vs. custom shelter comparison](/blog/prefab-vs-custom-storm-shelter/) walks through how the two approaches differ in practice.

## If You Still Plan to Build: Non-Negotiables

| Requirement | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Engineered plans matched to the 250 mph wind zone | Anything less isn't designed for what North Texas storms can produce |
| Foundation and anchorage sized to the plan, not a standard slab | Uplift and overturning loads will find any weak connection |
| A door assembly with an actual ICC 500 test report, installed exactly per the manufacturer's instructions | An untested or improperly installed door is a common weak point |
| Wall and roof materials matching the specified assembly (steel sheathing, reinforced concrete, or grouted masonry) | Plywood alone has no debris-impact rating |
| Ventilation sized for the shelter's rated occupancy | Needed for any stay longer than a few minutes |
| Soil and drainage assessment before any underground excavation | Clay soils in this area can collapse a trench or float a shell |
| A pulled permit before construction starts | Protects your rebate eligibility, insurance standing, and resale documentation |

If a build you're considering can't check every box on that table, talk it through with someone before you pour concrete or cut steel.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it legal to build my own storm shelter in Grayson County?

Building your own shelter isn't prohibited, but many cities require a permit before construction, and the rules differ between cities and unincorporated county land. Check with your local building department before you start, and see our [Texas storm shelter permits guide](/blog/storm-shelter-permits-texas/) for what that process usually involves.

### Can I use the free FEMA P-320 plans to build my own shelter?

The plans are public and free to download, but they're written for a qualified builder who can execute reinforced concrete, masonry, or steel-sheathed wood-frame construction to the exact specifications, including connections, anchorage, and a tested door assembly. Many homeowners find it more practical to have a professional handle the structural work even if they're managing other parts of the project themselves.

### Will a DIY shelter qualify for the Grayson County rebate?

It might, but only if it meets every criterion the county requires: engineered plans that satisfy FEMA and ICC 500 standards, an application and award letter obtained before construction, a location outside the mapped 100-year floodplain, and a passing site visit. A shelter without a permit, without a tested door assembly, or without documentation tying it to the standard is unlikely to pass review. Check with Grayson County OEM before you build if the rebate matters to your decision.

### Why can't I just use a solid exterior door instead of a special storm shelter door?

A standard exterior door, even a heavy one, hasn't been tested as a complete assembly against wind-borne debris. ICC 500 testing fires a 15 lb piece of lumber at 100 mph into the door, frame, and hardware together. A door that hasn't gone through that test as a full assembly is an unknown quantity in a real tornado, regardless of how solid it feels.

### Is an underground DIY shelter riskier than an above-ground one?

Underground builds add soil and drainage variables that above-ground shelters don't have, particularly in this area's expansive clay. An unshored excavation can collapse during construction, and a shelter that isn't properly anchored or ballasted can take on water or shift in saturated ground. That doesn't make underground shelters a bad choice, professionally installed ones handle this correctly, but it does raise the stakes on a DIY build.

### What's a safer alternative if I don't want to build from scratch?

A manufactured, factory-tested shelter is one option, since it comes with documentation tying it to an actual ICC 500 test report. Another is having a professional handle the parts of the project where mistakes matter most, the foundation, anchorage, and door, while you manage other aspects. Either route gives you something you can document for a rebate application, an insurance conversation, or a future buyer.

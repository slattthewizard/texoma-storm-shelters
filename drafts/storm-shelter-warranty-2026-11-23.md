# Storm Shelter Warranty: What to Look For Before You Sign

A storm shelter is a purchase you hope never to use for its real job, and one you'll live next to for decades. That's exactly why the warranty deserves a careful read. A shelter can sit in the yard for ten years looking fine, then develop a leaking seam, a sticking latch, or a rusted frame, and the warranty decides who pays to deal with it.

Here's the catch: "warranty" on a quote can mean very different things. One company may cover the steel shell and nothing else. Another may cover everything but only for the original owner. This guide walks through what to look for, what the fine print tends to exclude, and how to get the answers in writing before you commit.

We're not going to tell you how long a good warranty should run, because that varies by manufacturer and by installer. What matters more is what it covers, who's behind it, and what can quietly void it.

## A warranty is really three or four separate promises

When someone says "the shelter has a warranty," ask which part. Most shelters involve at least three parties or components, and each can carry its own coverage:

- **The structure.** The walls, roof, floor, welds, and seams of the shelter itself. For a steel unit that's the fabricated shell. For a poured or precast concrete unit it's the concrete work. This usually comes from the manufacturer.
- **The door and hardware.** Hinges, latches, seals, the door panel, and the frame. These are often made by a different company than the shelter body, and they wear out faster because they get used, slammed, and exposed to weather.
- **The workmanship and installation.** Anchoring, excavation, backfill, concrete pour, sealing, and finishing. This one comes from whoever installed the shelter, and it's often the shortest or the least clearly written.
- **Accessories and extras.** Vents, lighting, benches, hatches, drainage pumps. Sometimes covered, sometimes explicitly left out.

A quote that says "limited warranty" in one line tells you almost nothing. Ask for each of these in writing, with the name of the company that's responsible for it. If the manufacturer covers the shell and the installer covers the install, you should know that going in, because if the installer closes shop, the manufacturer's coverage may not reach installation problems.

## Structure coverage: what "structural" does and doesn't include

Structural warranties usually promise that the shelter body will be free of defects in materials and workmanship. That's a narrower promise than it sounds like. Read for these details:

**Defect versus damage.** A defect is a flaw in how the shelter was built, like a weld that cracks or a seam that was never sealed properly. Damage is something that happened to it afterward. Storm debris, a vehicle backing into it, or ground movement can all count as damage, and most warranties exclude them.

**What happens after a real storm.** Many warranties don't say what they do if a tornado actually hits the shelter. Ask directly: if the shelter takes a direct strike, is there any coverage, or does the warranty end at that point? Some owners rely on homeowner's insurance for that situation instead, so it's worth a call to your agent as well.

**The design standard.** A shelter built to FEMA P-320 criteria and tested to ICC 500 is designed for a 250 mph wind speed and a debris impact test (a 15 lb 2x4 at 100 mph). A warranty that promises the shelter will "perform as designed" is only worth something if the shelter was actually built and tested to a real standard. Our guides to [ICC 500 safe room requirements](/blog/icc-500-safe-room-requirements/) and [FEMA P-320 storm shelter standards](/blog/fema-p-320-storm-shelter-standards/) explain what to ask for. Remember that FEMA doesn't certify or approve products, so a claim of "FEMA-approved" on a warranty or brochure is a red flag in itself.

## Door and hardware coverage: where claims actually happen

The door is the part of a shelter that moves, so it's a natural place for problems to show up. Hinges bind, latches get stiff, seals dry out and crack, and the frame can shift slightly if the ground moves. Because the door is tested as a full assembly (door, frame, hinges, latches, and anchoring), the warranty should treat it that way too.

Ask these questions:

- Does the warranty cover the door, frame, hinges, latches, and seals, or only the door panel?
- Are seals and gaskets treated as wear items that are excluded after a short period?
- If a latch needs replacing, are replacement parts guaranteed to be tested hardware, or whatever's available?
- Who pays for labor to replace hardware, even if the part itself is covered?

That last question trips people up more than any other. Some warranties cover the part and leave the labor to the owner. For a heavy shelter door that can be a real expense. Our article on [storm shelter doors](/blog/storm-shelter-doors/) goes deeper on what a tested door assembly includes and what to inspect over time.

## Workmanship and installation: the part people forget to ask about

The shelter can be perfectly built and still fail because of how it was put in. A slab that's poorly anchored, an underground unit with a bad seal at the entry, or a drainage problem that lets water pool around a hatch are all installation issues, not manufacturing ones.

This is why the installer's workmanship coverage matters as much as the manufacturer's. A few things to pin down:

- **How long it runs and what it covers.** Anchoring, concrete, waterproofing, backfill, and grading should each be addressed, not lumped under a vague "workmanship" line.
- **Who responds.** Will the same crew come back? If the installer subcontracts the concrete or excavation, who's responsible for those parts?
- **Settling and ground movement.** Much of Grayson County sits on Blackland Prairie clay that shrinks and swells with moisture, and Cooke County is mostly Cross Timbers country with its own soil quirks. If a warranty excludes "settling" or "soil movement," ask exactly what that means here, and whether it applies to a unit that's been anchored for local conditions.

## Rust, corrosion, and water: the exclusions that matter most

For below-grade shelters especially, moisture is the long-term enemy. Steel left damp will eventually rust. Concrete can leak at joints. Hatches and entries are the usual weak points. So the exclusions around water and corrosion deserve more attention than the headline coverage.

Read for language like this:

- **"Surface rust is not a defect."** Light surface rust on a steel shelter is common and often cosmetic. But if the warranty excludes all rust, ask where the line is between surface rust and rust that compromises the steel.
- **"Water intrusion is excluded."** Some warranties exclude any water entering the shelter, period. Others cover leaks through the shelter body but not through the entry. Ask which parts are covered and whether drainage or a sump is part of the install.
- **Finish and coating.** Paint or powder coating that peels or fades is often excluded or covered for a much shorter time than the structure.
- **Maintenance conditions.** Some warranties require you to keep the shelter clean, ventilated, and free of standing water. If you skip that, coverage may be denied.

Our guides on [water in a storm shelter](/blog/water-in-storm-shelter/) and [storm shelter maintenance tips](/blog/storm-shelter-maintenance-tips/) explain what causes moisture problems and what regular upkeep looks like. If your warranty requires maintenance, keep notes and photos of what you do and when.

## Transferability: will it survive a sale?

A shelter is an asset that can matter to the next owner of your home. Whether the warranty follows the property or ends when you sell is worth knowing before you buy, not when you list the house.

Ask:

- Is the warranty transferable to a new owner of the property?
- Is there a fee, a form, or a time limit for transferring it?
- Does the new owner need to register it, and with whom?
- If the shelter was registered with the city or county emergency management, does that registration transfer too? See our note on [registering your storm shelter](/blog/register-your-storm-shelter/).

If the answer is "no, original owner only," that's not necessarily a dealbreaker, but it should factor into the price you're willing to pay.

## What can void a storm shelter warranty

Most warranties list conditions that cancel or reduce coverage. These tend to be reasonable on their face, but you want to know them up front:

- **Moving the shelter.** Relocating an above-ground unit, even a few feet, can void coverage, since the anchoring was done for one specific location. Underground units can't really be moved at all.
- **Modifications.** Cutting a new vent, welding on shelving, drilling through the walls, or swapping the door for something else can change a tested assembly. If you want to add anything, ask the installer first.
- **Unauthorized repairs.** If someone other than an approved installer does repair work, the warranty may no longer apply to that area.
- **Missed registration.** Some manufacturers require you to register the warranty within a certain period after install.
- **Not using it as intended.** Storing heavy items in a way that blocks the door, or using the shelter for something it wasn't designed for, can be cited against you.

None of this is unusual. Just don't assume. Ask for the voiding conditions as a list.

## A quick warranty checklist

Bring this to any estimate, and make sure the answers end up in writing, not just in conversation:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Who backs each part (shell, door, install)? | Tells you who to call, and what happens if one company goes away |
| What exactly is covered and for how long, by part? | Avoids a vague "limited warranty" with hidden gaps |
| Is labor covered, or only parts? | Labor can cost as much as the part |
| What's excluded for rust, water, and ground movement? | These are the most common long-term problems |
| Is it transferable, and what's the process? | Protects resale value |
| What voids it? | Prevents surprises from moving or modifying the shelter |
| How do I file a claim, and who answers? | A warranty is only useful if you can reach someone |

## Get it in writing, and keep it

Verbal promises don't survive a change in staff, ownership, or memory. Before you sign, ask for the full warranty document, not a summary, and read it at your own pace. Compare the actual wording against what the salesperson said. If they differ, the document is what counts.

Once you've signed, keep copies somewhere you won't lose them: a folder with the contract, the warranty, the installation date, any permit paperwork, and photos taken right after install. If you ever need to make a claim, that paperwork makes the process far easier.

Price matters too, of course. Typical residential shelters in North Texas run $4,500 to $9,500 installed, and our [storm shelter cost](/storm-shelter-cost/) page breaks down ranges by type. A cheaper quote with thin coverage isn't always cheaper in the long run, but a higher price doesn't guarantee better terms either. The paperwork is where you find out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a storm shelter warranty cover tornado damage?

Often not. Most warranties cover defects in materials and workmanship, not damage from storms, debris, or vehicles. Ask directly what happens if the shelter takes a direct hit, and check with your homeowner's insurance agent about what that policy covers for a shelter.

### Is a storm shelter warranty transferable if I sell my house?

It depends on the document. Some warranties follow the property, some require a form or a fee, and some end with the original owner. Ask before you buy, and get the transfer terms in writing, since a transferable warranty can help when you sell.

### Does the warranty cover rust or water leaks?

Usually only in part. Many warranties exclude surface rust and treat water intrusion differently depending on whether it comes through the shelter body or the entry. Read the exclusions closely, and ask whether drainage and sealing at the hatch or door are part of the installer's responsibility.

### What voids a storm shelter warranty?

Common causes include moving the shelter, modifying it (new openings, welds, or a different door), repairs by an unapproved party, and skipping required registration or upkeep. Ask for the voiding conditions as a written list so there are no surprises.

### Who handles the warranty, the manufacturer or the installer?

Often both, for different parts. The manufacturer typically stands behind the shelter body and sometimes the door, while the installer stands behind anchoring, concrete, and sealing. Get each party's coverage and contact information in writing so you know who to call.

### Should I register my warranty after installation?

If the paperwork asks for it, yes, and do it promptly. Some manufacturers require registration within a set period or the coverage may not apply. Also keep your contract, install date, and photos together in one place in case you need to file a claim later.

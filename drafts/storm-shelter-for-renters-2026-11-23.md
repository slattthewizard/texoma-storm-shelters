# Storm Shelter for Renters: Real Options in Texoma

If you rent, the usual advice about tornado safety can feel like it was written for someone else. "Install a safe room" is a fine suggestion when you own the slab it sits on. When the landlord owns the building, you can't just pour concrete or bolt a steel box into the garage.

You do have options, though. Some involve a conversation with your landlord, some involve places nearby that are already built for this, and some are simply a better plan for the space you have. This guide covers all of them, what to ask before you sign a lease in Texoma, and a section for landlords who are thinking about adding a shelter to a rental property.

## Start with the honest limits of renting

Many Texas homes are slab-on-grade with no basement, and apartments and duplexes are no different. That means there's usually no underground space to retreat to. A rented unit also may have shared walls, upper floors, or large windows, none of which helps in a tornado.

None of that means you're out of luck. It means your plan has to be more deliberate, because the default (staying put and hoping) is the one that leaves the most to chance. North Texas has tornadoes in every month, with a main peak in spring and a smaller bump in fall, so the plan isn't a once-a-year project.

## Option 1: Ask your landlord

This is the most direct route, and it's worth more than a text message. A well-prepared request is easier to say yes to.

Before you ask, decide what you're actually asking for. There's a big difference between these:

- **A shelter the landlord installs and owns.** The landlord pays, the shelter stays with the property.
- **A shelter you pay for, with written permission.** You cover some or all of the cost, and the two of you agree in writing what happens when you move out.
- **Access to an existing shelter or reinforced space.** Some older rentals have a cellar or a storage area the landlord hasn't thought about as a refuge.

If you're proposing a shelter, get everything in writing: who owns it, who pays for maintenance, what happens if you move, and whether the landlord needs a permit. A shelter is a permanent structure, so check with your city or county building department about what's needed before anyone breaks ground. Our guide to [storm shelter permits in Texas](/blog/storm-shelter-permits-texas/) is a good place to start.

Be realistic. A landlord might say no, or might say yes only if you cover the cost. Costs for a residential unit typically run $4,500 to $9,500 installed in North Texas, and our [storm shelter cost](/storm-shelter-cost/) page breaks that down by type. A smaller 4-6 person above-ground unit runs $4,500 to $6,200, and a garage in-floor unit runs $4,800 to $7,900. For a tenant, spending that on something you can't take with you is a hard sell, which is why the other options below matter.

## Option 2: Find a community shelter near you

Community shelters (built to FEMA P-361 criteria) exist so that people without a safe room at home have somewhere to go. Availability varies a lot by town and neighborhood, and some are open only during certain events or to certain groups, such as a school's students and staff.

Here's how to find out what's near you:

- **Call your city or county emergency management office.** Ask whether there are public shelters in your area, who can use them, and whether they're open during a warning or only by arrangement.
- **Ask your apartment complex or mobile home park.** Some larger properties have a designated shelter or reinforced space for residents. If yours does, learn exactly where it is and how to get in at night.
- **Check with schools, churches, and workplaces.** Some have shelter space and some don't. If one does, ask whether it's open to the public and under what conditions.

The key point is timing. A tornado warning can give you very little lead time, so a shelter you can't reach in a few minutes isn't a plan. If the nearest option is a drive away, you'd have to leave well before a storm arrives, and driving in the open during severe weather carries its own risks. Our article on [what to do if a tornado threatens while you're driving](/blog/what-to-do-tornado-while-driving/) explains why that matters.

## Option 3: Arrange access to a neighbor's shelter

Some homeowners in Sherman, Denison, Pottsboro, Van Alstyne and the surrounding towns have a storm shelter in the yard or garage, and some would gladly share it. If you get along with a neighbor, it's worth asking before the season starts.

If you do, talk through the details in advance:

- How many people does the shelter hold, and how many will already be in it? Our [storm shelter size guide](/blog/storm-shelter-size-guide/) explains capacity, and a small unit fills quickly.
- How will you get in? A shelter that's locked or needs a key isn't much good at 2 a.m.
- What about pets? Some owners are fine with it, some aren't. Our guide to [pet safety in a storm shelter](/blog/pet-safety-storm-shelter/) covers the practical side.
- When should you head over? Agree on a trigger, such as a tornado warning for your county, so nobody is guessing.

An informal arrangement isn't a guarantee. The owner might be away, and the unit might be full. Treat a neighbor's offer as one layer of your plan, not the whole thing.

## Option 4: Make a plan for the best interior space you have

When there's no shelter available, the goal shifts to getting as much protection as the building allows. Guidance from the National Weather Service is consistent on the basics: get to the lowest floor, move to a small interior room, and put as many walls as you can between you and the outside. A bathroom, closet, or interior hallway with no windows is generally better than a room with exterior walls.

Keep these points in mind if you rent:

- **Ground floor beats upper floors.** If you live above the first floor, plan to go down to the lowest level, to an interior hall or stairwell, rather than staying where you are. Ask your property manager which areas of the building are the safest.
- **Stay away from windows and large open spans.** Big living rooms with wide ceilings and lots of glass are the worst places to be.
- **Cover up.** A mattress, couch cushions, or a heavy blanket over you can help against flying debris. Helmets (bike or sports) are a cheap and sensible addition for everyone in the household.
- **Know what to grab.** Keep shoes, a flashlight, a phone charger, and any medications near your refuge space. Our [emergency kit for a storm shelter](/blog/emergency-kit-storm-shelter/) article lists the basics.

For more on room selection, see our guide to the [safest place in a tornado](/blog/safest-place-in-a-tornado/), which also covers what to do if you live in a mobile home or apartment.

Be clear-eyed about it: an interior room is not a safe room. It's the best use of what you have when nothing better is available, and it's much better than the open living room. But it isn't tested to ICC 500, and a strong tornado can destroy a whole building.

## Questions to ask before signing a lease in Texoma

If you're looking for a rental, you can build tornado safety into your search. Ask the landlord or property manager these questions before you sign:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Is there a storm shelter, cellar, or designated safe area on the property? | If yes, you want to know where and how to access it |
| Who has the key or access code, and is it available at night? | A locked shelter doesn't help when a warning hits |
| Is the unit on the ground floor, and is there an interior room with no windows? | Your fallback plan depends on this |
| Is the building a mobile or manufactured home? | These are especially vulnerable; see our [mobile home storm shelter](/blog/mobile-home-storm-shelter/) guide |
| Would you allow a shelter if I paid for it, and what happens if I move? | Opens the conversation and sets terms in writing |
| Does the property have a siren, weather radio, or alert system for residents? | Extra warning time helps |
| What's the complex's policy on pets during severe weather? | Pets are family, and rules can differ |

Don't forget your own alerts. Wireless Emergency Alerts push tornado warnings to your phone with no app or sign-up, and a NOAA Weather Radio with the right county code (Grayson 048181, Fannin 048147, Cooke 048097) gives you a backup when cell service fails. See our guides on [tornado alerts on your phone](/blog/tornado-alerts-on-your-phone/) and [NOAA Weather Radio in Texoma](/blog/noaa-weather-radio-texoma/).

## For landlords: should you add a shelter to a rental?

If you own rental property, a shelter is something many rentals don't have. It can be a selling point for tenants and a real safety feature for the people living there. Here are a few things to weigh.

**Which type fits the property.** For a single-family rental or duplex with a garage, a [garage floor shelter](/garage-floor-shelters/) or an [above-ground safe room](/above-ground-safe-rooms/) is usually the simplest fit. A larger multi-unit property may need something bigger, or a community-style unit built to FEMA P-361 criteria. Our [commercial storm shelter](/commercial-storm-shelters/) page covers those, with costs that range from $15,000 to $75,000 or more.

**Rebates.** The Grayson County safe room rebate has covered 50% of cost up to $3,000 when funded, and it requires pre-approval before installation. It runs periodically and eligibility rules apply, so don't assume it's available. If your rental is outside Grayson County, ask your county emergency management office whether a program is open. Our guide to the [Grayson County storm shelter rebate](/blog/grayson-county-storm-shelter-rebate/) has details.

**Legal questions.** Whether a landlord is required to provide a shelter, what disclosures apply, and how a shelter affects your lease, insurance, or liability are questions for a lawyer. We can't answer them, and this article isn't legal advice. Talk to an attorney and your insurance agent before you decide, and check with your city or county building department on permits.

**Maintenance and access.** A shelter is only useful if tenants can reach it. Think about who holds the key, who checks the seals and latches, and how new tenants learn it's there. Our [maintenance tips](/blog/storm-shelter-maintenance-tips/) cover what upkeep looks like.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I install a storm shelter in a rental home?

Only with your landlord's written permission, since a shelter is a permanent addition to the property. Agree in writing on who pays, who owns it, and what happens when you move out. Also check with your city or county building department about permits.

### What should I do if my apartment has no shelter?

Know where the nearest community shelter is, and find out whether it's open to the public during warnings. If there isn't one close enough to reach quickly, plan to move to the lowest floor and an interior room away from windows, and cover yourself with a mattress or heavy blankets.

### Are there community storm shelters in Texoma?

Some towns and properties have them, but availability and access rules vary. Call your city or county emergency management office and ask which shelters are open to the public, when, and who can use them. Don't wait until a warning is issued to find out.

### Is a closet or bathroom really safe in a tornado?

It's the best option when you don't have a shelter, because interior rooms put more walls between you and the outside. But it isn't a tested safe room, and a strong tornado can destroy the building. Get to the lowest floor, stay away from windows, and cover your head and body.

### Can I take a storm shelter with me when I move?

Underground and in-floor shelters can't be moved, and above-ground units are anchored to a specific location, which can affect any warranty if you relocate them. If you pay for a shelter in a rental, settle in writing what happens to it when you leave.

### Do landlords have to provide a storm shelter in Texas?

We can't give legal advice on that. Obligations depend on the lease, the property, and the law, so talk to an attorney before you make assumptions about what a landlord must or mustn't do.

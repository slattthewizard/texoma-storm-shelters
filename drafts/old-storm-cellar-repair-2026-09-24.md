# Old Storm Cellar? How to Inspect It and When to Repair, Upgrade or Replace

A lot of older farmhouses and rural lots around Sherman, Denison and the rest of Texoma have a storm cellar someone dug decades ago, usually poured concrete or concrete block with a heavy door set into a slope or a flat lid at ground level. It isn't the same thing as a manufactured storm shelter. That difference matters when you're deciding whether it'll still do its job. Below is a room-by-room way to inspect an old cellar, plus a straight answer on when to repair it, when to upgrade it, and when it's time to replace or retire it.

## Storm Cellar vs. Storm Shelter: What's the Difference?

If a previous owner or a family member dug a hole and poured concrete over it sometime in the 60s, 70s or earlier, that was built to local custom and whatever that builder knew at the time. There was no test standard behind it, no engineer signing off on the door, and no inspection when it went in.

A modern storm shelter is a different category of product. It's designed and built to FEMA P-320 criteria for a residential safe room (FEMA P-361 covers community and commercial safe rooms), and it's tested to ICC 500, the consensus standard for storm shelter design, construction and testing. Part of that testing is a debris impact test: a 15 lb 2x4 fired at the walls and doors at 100 mph, and at the roof at 67 mph. The door is tested as a complete assembly, meaning the door panel, frame, hinges and latches all have to hold together, not just the steel or fiberglass panel by itself.

That doesn't mean every old cellar is unsafe, or that every tested shelter is perfect. It means an old cellar was never built or tested against that standard, so you evaluate it on its own, piece by piece, instead of assuming it performs the way a rated shelter would. And FEMA doesn't certify, approve or rate shelters or installers itself. Whatever a listing claims, what you actually want to see is an ICC 500 test report or a manufacturer's statement of compliance, and for anything involving the county, an answer on what they require.

## A Room-by-Room Inspection Guide for an Old Cellar

Before storm season, or before you decide what to do with a cellar you inherited with the property, walk through each of these in order. Bring a flashlight, wear boots, and don't go in if you can smell standing water or see obvious structural movement until someone's had a look at it. Our [storm shelter inspection process](/blog/storm-shelter-inspection-process/) guide covers the same walkthrough for a newer shelter, if you want the fuller version.

### The Door

The door is usually the weak point on an old cellar. Check it first. Look for:

- Wood doors that have softened, split or delaminated, or steel doors with rust through the panel, not just surface rust.
- Hinges that are rusted stiff, loose in the frame, or missing hardware.
- An out-swing lid or door, meaning it opens outward and upward. On older cellars this was common, but it means storm debris, a fallen limb or even blown dirt can pile up and pin the door shut from outside, trapping whoever is inside.
- A latch or handle that doesn't work smoothly from both sides. You need to be able to get out without fighting the door.

A door that fails on any of these points is the single most common reason an otherwise sound old cellar isn't safe to rely on. See our [guide to storm shelter doors](/blog/storm-shelter-doors/) for what a properly rated door and frame assembly actually looks like.

### Roof Slab: Cracks and Exposed Rebar

Look at the underside of the roof slab for cracking. Hairline surface cracks with no movement are common in old concrete and usually cosmetic. What you're watching for is different: cracks wide enough to fit a coin, cracks that have visibly grown since the last time you looked, or rebar that's exposed and rusting. Rusting rebar expands as it corrodes, which pushes the surrounding concrete apart and makes the crack worse over time. That's a structural issue, not a cosmetic one.

### Walls and Water Staining

Run your hand along the walls. Efflorescence (a white, chalky mineral residue) or dark water staining tells you moisture is getting through the concrete or block, even if the cellar looks dry the day you check it. Note where the staining is heaviest, usually it points back to the source, whether that's a crack, a poor seal around the door frame, or grading outside that sheds water toward the cellar instead of away from it.

### Steps and Handrail

Old cellar steps are often narrow, uneven, or missing a handrail entirely. In an emergency, people will be moving fast, sometimes in the dark, sometimes with kids or pets. Loose steps, crumbling edges, or no handrail turn a fifteen-second trip down into a fall risk.

### Ventilation

Check whether the cellar has any vent at all, and if it does, whether it's still open and clear of dirt, leaves or a wasp nest. A sealed, unvented cellar can get stale fast with several people in it, and in wet ground, poor ventilation makes the moisture problem worse. Our [storm shelter ventilation guide](/blog/storm-shelter-ventilation/) covers what adequate airflow looks like for a below-ground space.

### Standing Water

Water on the floor, even an inch or two, changes the calculation. Occasional dampness after a heavy rain is a drainage problem you can often fix. Water that's there most of the time, or that comes back within a day or two of pumping it out, points to a bigger issue: a high water table, poor grading, or a crack that's actively letting groundwater in. See [water in your storm shelter](/blog/water-in-storm-shelter/) for how to tell the difference and what each situation calls for.

### Snakes, Wasps and Other Uninvited Tenants

A cellar that sits closed most of the year is an attractive spot for snakes, wasps, mice and spiders. Before you assume the cellar is ready to use, open it up, let it air out, and check corners and gaps around pipe penetrations or the door frame. This is a simple check but it gets skipped more often than any other item on this list.

### Can You Get Out From the Inside?

Last and most important: with the door closed, can someone actually open it from inside without a fight? Test the latch, test the hinges, and if the door is heavy or stiff, treat that as a safety issue to fix now, not later.

## Repair, Upgrade or Replace: A Decision Table

Not every issue on an old cellar means starting over. Sort what you find into one of the buckets below.

| What you find | What it usually means | Likely path |
|---|---|---|
| Solid door, hinges tight, latch works from both sides | Structure is sound | Minor maintenance, weatherstripping, lubricate hardware |
| Wood door that's split or soft, or an out-swing lid that debris could pin shut | The door is the weak link | Upgrade to a rated replacement door and frame |
| Hairline surface cracks, no water, no movement | Normal aging | Patch and monitor at each season change |
| Cracks wide enough for a coin, cracks that keep growing, or exposed rusting rebar | Structural cracking | Have the slab and walls evaluated before spending on anything else |
| Damp after heavy rain but dries out within a day or two | A drainage issue | Fix grading, add a sump, or seal specific entry points |
| Standing water most of the year, or the shell has visibly shifted | Chronic flooding or soil movement | Replace, or consider a shelter type suited to your soil and drainage |
| No working release or handle from the inside | Life-safety issue on its own | Fix or replace immediately, regardless of anything else on this list |

If the shell of the cellar (the walls and roof slab) is structurally sound and the only problems are the door, hardware, ventilation or minor drainage, a repair or a door upgrade is usually the right call and the cheaper one. If you're finding structural cracking in the concrete, chronic standing water, or a shell that's shifted in the ground, that's usually a sign the cellar has reached the end of its useful life and a new, properly engineered shelter is the better long-term answer. Our [underground storm shelters](/underground-storm-shelters/) page covers the options that hold up better against the clay soils common across Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties. Pricing on a new install depends heavily on soil conditions, site access and the type of shelter, so we'd rather give you an accurate number after a look at the site than a figure that doesn't apply to your property; you can see typical ranges on our [storm shelter cost](/storm-shelter-cost/) page as a starting point.

If a cellar is beyond saving and you decide not to replace it, don't just leave a hole in the ground. An abandoned cellar should be filled and the site graded properly so it isn't a collapse hazard for people, pets or livestock.

## Using an Old Cellar as a Root Cellar

If the structure checks out but you'd rather not rely on it as your storm shelter, using it as a root cellar for canned goods, potatoes or garden storage is a reasonable second life for it. The main thing to watch is access: don't let shelving, bins or stored goods block the path to the door. If severe weather does roll through while you're using it for storage, you want to be able to get in and move around without tripping over crates in the dark.

## What About the Grayson County Rebate?

Grayson County's Office of Emergency Management runs a Residential Safe Room Rebate Program that can reimburse part of the cost of an eligible safe room for homes outside the mapped 100-year floodplain. An old, owner-built cellar generally won't qualify on its own, since it wasn't built or tested to FEMA and ICC 500 criteria, and you have to apply and get county approval before installation starts. Program details and funding availability change, so check with the county directly if the rebate factors into your decision. Our [Grayson County storm shelter rebate guide](/blog/grayson-county-storm-shelter-rebate/) covers the process; Fannin and Cooke County homeowners should ask their own county emergency management office whether anything similar is running.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a storm cellar and a storm shelter?

A storm cellar is typically an older, owner-built structure put in without a formal design standard behind it. A storm shelter is designed and built to FEMA P-320 or P-361 criteria and tested to ICC 500, which means the door, walls and roof have all been verified against wind and debris impact requirements.

### Can an old storm cellar be repaired instead of replaced?

Often, yes. If the concrete shell is structurally sound and the problems are limited to the door, hardware, ventilation or minor drainage, a repair or door upgrade usually solves it. Structural cracking, chronic standing water or a shell that has shifted in the ground are the signs that point toward replacement instead.

### Will my old storm cellar qualify for the Grayson County rebate?

Generally not on its own, since the rebate requires the shelter to meet FEMA and ICC 500 criteria and requires county approval before installation. Check with the county's Office of Emergency Management directly to confirm current requirements before you decide between repairing and replacing.

### Is it safe to use an old storm cellar as a root cellar?

If the structure is sound, storing food and garden goods in it is a reasonable use. Just keep the path to the door clear so you can get in and out easily if you ever need to use it for shelter instead.

### How do I know if my storm cellar door needs to be replaced?

Check for rust through the panel rather than just surface rust, a wood door that's split or soft, hinges that are stiff or loose, and whether it's an out-swing door that debris could pile against and pin shut. If the latch doesn't work smoothly from inside, treat that as an immediate fix.

### What should I do if my storm cellar keeps flooding?

Occasional dampness after heavy rain usually points to a drainage or grading fix. Standing water that's there most of the time, or that returns within a day or two of pumping it out, usually means a bigger issue with the water table or a crack that's letting groundwater in, and you should get that evaluated before deciding whether to repair or replace the cellar.

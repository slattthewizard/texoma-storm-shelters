# What to Do After a Tornado: A Step-by-Step Guide for the First Hours

The warning has expired, the wind has dropped, and you're sitting in your shelter wondering whether it's safe to open the door. The next hour matters almost as much as the storm itself. Debris, broken glass, leaking gas and fallen power lines turn the aftermath into its own set of hazards, and plenty of injuries happen to people who walk out too fast.

This guide covers what to do from the moment you leave your shelter, in roughly the order you'll need it. It's general safety guidance in line with what emergency agencies recommend. When anything here conflicts with instructions from local emergency officials, follow the officials.

## Before you leave the shelter

Don't rush. Take a minute to make sure the danger is really over.

- **Wait for the all-clear.** Check your weather radio or phone to confirm the warning has expired or been cancelled. Storms often come in groups, and a second cell can follow the first.
- **Listen first.** If you can hear wind, hail or thunder close by, stay put a little longer.
- **Count heads.** Make sure everyone, including pets, is accounted for before anyone moves.
- **Get your shoes and gear on.** Sturdy closed-toe shoes, long pants if you have them, and gloves protect you from glass, nails and sharp metal. A helmet helps if debris is still falling.
- **Open the door slowly.** Debris can pile up against it, especially on an out-swinging door. If it won't open, don't panic. Call out, use your phone to call 911 and give your location, and use a whistle if you have one.

If you're stuck inside and no one can reach you, stay calm and conserve your phone battery. A well-stocked shelter helps here. Our [emergency kit guide](/blog/emergency-kit-storm-shelter/) lists what to keep inside, and [storm shelter supplies](/blog/storm-shelter-supplies/) goes into more detail.

## Step 1: Check for injuries and call 911

Look at yourself and the people with you before anything else. Check for bleeding, broken bones, head injuries and anyone who's confused or unresponsive.

For anything serious, call 911 right away. Be ready to give your address, the number of people hurt and what kind of injuries you see. During a widespread event, phone lines may be busy, so if a call won't go through, try a text message or try again. Keep calls short so lines stay available for others.

Beyond calling for help, stick to basic first aid common sense. Apply firm pressure to a bleeding wound with a clean cloth. Don't move someone with a possible head or neck injury unless they're in immediate danger. Don't try to remove a large object embedded in someone. Wait for trained responders.

If you're able, check on your neighbors once your own household is safe. We'll come back to that below.

## Step 2: Check your surroundings before you walk anywhere

Once everyone's accounted for, look around from the doorway before you step out. The ground after a tornado can be a minefield.

**Broken glass and nails.** Boards with nails, shattered windows, and torn metal are everywhere in a debris field. Wear heavy shoes, watch where each step lands, and don't let children or pets wander. A puncture wound is easy to get and easy to ignore, so if you step on something sharp, get it looked at.

**Unstable structures and debris piles.** Walls, chimneys, roofs and trees can look fine and fall later, especially if the wind picks up again. Keep a safe distance from anything that looks like it could shift.

**Dust and insulation.** Debris can kick up fine particles. Cover your nose and mouth if you're working in a damaged area.

**Hail and standing water.** Slippery ground, flooded low spots and ice can add to the hazard.

## Step 3: Smell for gas and watch for downed power lines

These two hazards are still dangerous long after the wind has passed, so give them serious attention.

### Gas

If you smell gas, hear a hissing sound or suspect a leak, get everyone out and away from the building immediately. Don't turn lights on or off, don't use a lighter or match, don't run a generator indoors, and don't use a phone or flip switches inside the building. Once you're a safe distance away, call 911 and your gas utility. Don't go back in until a professional says it's safe.

### Downed power lines

Treat every fallen line as live, even if it looks dead or isn't sparking. Stay well back, keep children and pets away, and don't touch anything that's in contact with a line, such as a fence, a car or a puddle. Call 911 and your electric utility to report it. Don't try to move a line with a stick or tool.

If a line has fallen on a vehicle you're in, the usual advice is to stay inside and wait for help unless there's a fire. Our [power outage in a storm shelter](/blog/power-outage-shelter/) article also covers what to expect if the grid is down for a while.

### Water and other utilities

If you see broken water pipes or flooding, and you know where your main shutoff is, you can turn it off. Don't wade through water near downed lines, and don't use electrical equipment while standing in water.

## Step 4: Stay out of damaged buildings

It's tempting to rush back inside to grab belongings or check on pets. If your home has visible structural damage, such as a missing roof, shifted walls, a sagging ceiling, a cracked foundation or a leaning chimney, don't go in until a qualified professional or local authority has checked it.

Watch for these warning signs:

- Doors or windows that are jammed or out of square.
- A roofline that sags or has visible gaps.
- Walls that lean, bulge or have large cracks.
- Smells of gas or sounds of hissing.
- Water or electrical damage inside.

If you must go in briefly for something urgent, like medication, tell someone, go in daylight if possible, wear sturdy shoes and a hard hat or helmet, and keep it short. Never use candles or open flames inside a damaged building. Use a flashlight.

If you can't stay in your home, you'll need somewhere else to go. Local emergency management and relief organizations often set up information and shelter resources after a major event, so check official local channels for details.

## Step 5: Document the damage for your insurance claim

Once it's safe, start recording. Insurance companies want evidence, and memories fade quickly.

1. **Photograph and video everything** before you move or clean anything. Take wide shots of each room and the exterior, then close-ups of specific damage.
2. **Make a list** of damaged or lost items with approximate age, brand and value if you remember it.
3. **Keep receipts** for any emergency repairs, hotel stays, meals or supplies you buy because of the storm.
4. **Protect what you can.** Covering a damaged roof with a tarp and boarding broken windows is generally expected to prevent further loss. Keep receipts and photos of the work.
5. **Call your insurer promptly.** Ask what's covered, what the deadlines are and whether an adjuster needs to see the damage before repairs start.
6. **Keep records in one place**, such as a folder or a shared cloud album, and write down the date, time and name every time you speak with someone.

Be cautious with anyone who knocks on your door right after a storm offering immediate repairs, especially if they ask for money up front. Take your time, get more than one written estimate, and check with your city or county building department about permit requirements before major work begins.

## Step 6: Check the shelter itself

This step gets skipped, and it shouldn't. After a major storm, your shelter may have taken hits you can't see from the inside. Once the area is safe, walk around the shelter and look it over.

- **The door and frame.** Check for dents, bends, gaps or a door that doesn't close and latch normally. A damaged frame can keep the door from sealing. Our guide to [storm shelter doors](/blog/storm-shelter-doors/) explains what to look for.
- **The latches and hinges.** Test each latch point. If something feels loose, bent or stiff, don't ignore it.
- **Water.** Look for standing water around an underground unit, leaks or a wet floor inside. Our article on [water in a storm shelter](/blog/water-in-storm-shelter/) covers causes and fixes.
- **Vents.** Make sure ventilation openings aren't blocked by debris. See [storm shelter ventilation](/blog/storm-shelter-ventilation/) for what to check.
- **Anchoring.** On an above-ground unit, look for any sign the shelter has shifted, or that the anchoring or slab has cracked.
- **Debris on top.** On an underground hatch or garage lid, clear anything that's landed on it so it still opens.

If you find damage, contact the installer or manufacturer. For routine upkeep when nothing's wrong, our [maintenance tips](/blog/storm-shelter-maintenance-tips/) cover what to inspect each season, and our [warranty guide](/blog/storm-shelter-warranty/) helps if you need to ask about a claim.

## Step 7: Help your neighbors, safely

After you've checked yourself and your own household, check on people nearby, especially older neighbors, anyone who lives alone, and people with limited mobility or small children.

- Knock, call or text before entering anyone's home.
- Don't move seriously injured people unless they're in immediate danger. Call 911.
- Share what you know about gas, lines and damaged structures with others on the street.
- Offer a charged phone, a flashlight or a place to sit while you wait for help.
- Leave heavy cleanup and debris removal to people who have the right equipment, unless the situation is urgent.

A quick rule is to help in ways that don't put you at more risk. Rescuers can't help you if you become another casualty.

## Step 8: Plan for the next few days

Once the immediate danger passes, the practical stuff begins. Keep your phone charged, listen to local officials for updates, and keep a small supply of water, food and medications on hand. Be careful with generators, which should only run outdoors and away from windows and doors. If your power's out, avoid candles when possible and use flashlights.

It's also worth reviewing your plan. Did the alerts reach you? Did everyone know what to do? Was your shelter or safe spot easy to reach? If the answer to any of those was no, now's a good time to fix it. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should I wait before leaving my storm shelter?

Wait until the tornado warning has expired or been cancelled, and until the wind and hail have clearly died down. Check a weather radio or phone for the latest alerts, since another storm cell can follow the first. When you do open the door, do it slowly in case debris is piled against it.

### What's the first thing I should do after a tornado?

Check yourself and everyone with you for injuries, and call 911 for anything serious. Put on sturdy shoes and gloves before you walk through debris. Once your household is safe, look for gas smells, downed lines and unstable structures before you move around.

### Is it safe to go back into my house after a tornado?

Only if it shows no signs of structural damage and you don't smell gas. If the roof, walls, ceiling or foundation look damaged, stay out until a qualified professional or local authority says it's safe. If you have to go in briefly, wear sturdy shoes and use a flashlight, never an open flame.

### What should I photograph for my insurance claim?

Take wide photos and video of every room and the exterior, then close-ups of each damaged area, before you move or clean anything. Keep a list of damaged items and every receipt for emergency repairs or lodging. Call your insurer promptly and ask about their requirements.

### How do I know my storm shelter is still safe to use?

Inspect the door, frame, latches, seals, vents and anchoring once the area is safe. Look for dents, bent hardware, standing water and debris on top of hatches or lids. If anything looks damaged or doesn't work normally, contact your installer or the manufacturer before relying on the shelter again.

### What should I do if I smell gas after a storm?

Leave the building immediately with everyone in your household, and don't flip switches, use a lighter or run a generator inside. Once you're a safe distance away, call 911 and your gas utility. Don't go back in until a professional confirms it's safe.

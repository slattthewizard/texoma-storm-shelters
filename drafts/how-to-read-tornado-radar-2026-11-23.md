# How to Read Tornado Radar: Hook Echoes, Velocity Couplets and What They Can't Tell You

Radar apps put a forecaster's tool in everyone's hands. That's useful, and it's also risky. A little knowledge about what the colors mean can help you understand why a warning was issued. A little too much confidence can talk you out of taking cover. This guide explains the two views that matter most, what the classic tornado signatures look like, where radar goes blind, and why the warning is always the thing to act on.

## Radar sees precipitation, not tornadoes

A weather radar sends out a pulse of energy and listens for what bounces back. Raindrops, hail and debris all reflect it. The radar doesn't "see" a tornado the way a camera would. It sees what's inside and around a storm, and meteorologists interpret the patterns.

Two products do most of the work for tornado spotting: reflectivity and velocity. Radar apps usually show reflectivity by default. Velocity is often hidden one tap away, and it's the more important one for rotation.

## Reflectivity: how much is out there

Reflectivity shows how much energy came back, which roughly corresponds to how heavy the precipitation is. It's the familiar green, yellow, red and sometimes purple map. Light rain shows up as green and the heaviest rain or hail shows up as red, magenta or white.

Reflectivity is good at showing the shape of a storm. A few features matter:

- **A hook echo.** On a supercell thunderstorm, the rain curls around the rotating area and forms a hook shape, often on the southwest side of the main storm. A hook doesn't prove a tornado exists. It's a sign of strong rotation in the storm, and it has long been a clue forecasters watch for.
- **A debris ball.** This is a small, compact blob of returns at the tip of a hook. When a tornado is on the ground and lofting debris, the radar can pick up the pieces, such as dirt, leaves and parts of buildings. A debris ball is a strong sign that damage is happening. Modern radar uses an extra product, often called correlation coefficient, to confirm that the objects are not rain. That's how forecasters can say a tornado is on the ground even when no one can see it.
- **Inflow notches and bows.** Other shapes, such as an indentation or a bowing line, point to different wind threats, like straight-line wind. Our guide on [tornado vs straight line winds](/blog/tornado-vs-straight-line-winds/) explains why those matter.

The hook is a clue, not a verdict. Plenty of tornadoes occur without a clear hook, and some hooks never produce one. That's especially true in rain-wrapped storms, where heavy precipitation hides the structure.

## Velocity: which way the air is moving

Velocity shows motion relative to the radar. Instead of intensity, the colors tell you whether the wind is moving toward the radar or away from it. By convention, green means motion toward the radar, and red means motion away from it. Brighter, more saturated colors mean faster movement.

The pattern to know is the **velocity couplet**, sometimes called a rotation signature. When bright green and bright red sit right next to each other, the air is moving in opposite directions across a very small distance. That's rotation. The tighter and brighter the couplet, the stronger the rotation.

Some points that help you read it without overreading it:

- A broad, weak area of green next to red can be a wide circulation that isn't tornadic.
- A small, tight, intense couplet, especially one that persists over several radar scans, is more concerning.
- Velocity shows motion toward or away from the radar only. A storm directly beside the radar can show rotation poorly, and distance affects how well small features resolve.
- Velocity data can look noisy, with speckled colors that don't mean anything. Trained eyes tell real couplets from noise.

Forecasters pair these views. A hook on reflectivity at the same location as a tight couplet on velocity is the kind of combination that leads to a tornado warning. Add a debris ball and the warning wording gets more urgent. For the top end of that scale, see our guide to [tornado emergencies and PDS wording](/blog/tornado-emergency-vs-pds/).

## Radar has gaps you can't see on the screen

Radar looks like a complete picture. It isn't. Three limits are worth knowing, in plain terms.

**The beam climbs as it travels.** A radar beam travels in a nearly straight line, but the Earth curves away beneath it. The farther a storm is from the radar site, the higher in the sky the beam is when it reaches the storm. At long range the beam can pass over the lowest part of a storm, which is where a tornado actually is. A storm that looks unimpressive on screen may have rotation underneath that the beam overshoots.

**There's a cone of silence near the radar.** The radar can't see directly above itself, so storms right next to the site may be partly missed.

**Terrain, trees and other objects can block or distort returns.** And the radar is a snapshot. Each scan takes a few minutes, so a tornado can form, strengthen and cross a road between updates. What you're looking at is slightly in the past.

Much of Texoma sits a fair distance from the nearest NWS radar sites, so the lowest part of a storm here may be below the beam. Forecasters know this and account for it. You can't from an app.

## Why radar is a supplement, not a substitute for the warning

The National Weather Service issues tornado warnings based on radar, spotter reports, and other information. Local forecasters see more data than a phone app shows, and they know the storm's history, the local gaps and what the signatures mean in context. A free app gives you a fraction of that.

So the rule is simple: if a tornado warning is issued for your area, take cover. Don't wait to see whether the radar on your phone agrees. Don't decide a warning is a false alarm because you can't find a hook. Don't decide you're safe because the red couplet looks like it's passing to the north. Radar apps can also be delayed, and the picture can lag behind the real storm.

Radar is useful before the warning, too. A glance at the screen can tell you a line of storms is approaching and you have time to get kids and pets ready, charge devices and move the cars into the garage. ## Don't chase, and don't go out to look

Learning to read radar is a good way to understand storms. It's a bad reason to go toward one. Chasing is dangerous even for professionals. Tornadoes can change direction and speed suddenly, they can be wrapped in rain so you can't see them, and the roads fill with other drivers. Don't drive toward a storm to confirm what you saw on a screen.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does a hook echo on radar mean?

A hook echo is a curl of precipitation on the southwest side of a supercell, wrapped around a rotating area. It's a sign of strong rotation and often shows up before or during a tornado. It doesn't guarantee one, and tornadoes can happen without a visible hook.

### What is a debris ball on radar?

A debris ball is a small, tight cluster of returns at the tip of a hook, caused by debris lofted into the air. Forecasters confirm it by checking that the returns aren't rain. It's a strong indicator that a tornado is on the ground and causing damage.

### What do the red and green colors mean on velocity radar?

Green shows air moving toward the radar and red shows air moving away. When bright green and bright red sit side by side in a small area, the air is rotating, which is called a velocity couplet. Brighter colors mean faster motion.

### Can I rely on a radar app instead of waiting for a warning?

No. Apps show only part of the data forecasters use, can lag behind the storm, and can't show everything near the ground at long range. Treat the warning as the decision point and take cover when one is issued for your area.

### Why can radar miss a tornado?

The radar beam rises as it moves away from the site, so at long range it may pass over the low levels of a storm where a tornado forms. Scans also take a few minutes, so fast changes can happen between updates. Rain-wrapped storms can hide features too.

### Should I watch the radar to decide if a warning applies to me?

Use radar to understand what's happening, but don't use it to talk yourself out of shelter. If a warning is issued for your area, go to your safest spot first. You can look at the radar once you're there.

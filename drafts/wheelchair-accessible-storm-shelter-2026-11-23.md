# Wheelchair Accessible Storm Shelter: What to Look For

Most storm shelter advice assumes everyone can walk down a flight of stairs in the dark, quickly, with a warning blaring. That's not true for a lot of households. If someone in your home uses a wheelchair or walker, has limited stamina, or just can't manage steps safely, the shelter you choose has to work for them first. A shelter you can't reach in time isn't doing its job.

The good news is that accessible options exist. The key is knowing which questions to ask and which styles fit best.

## Start with the route, not just the shelter

Before you compare models, think about the whole trip. How long does it take to get from where each person usually is, bedroom, living room, kitchen, to the shelter door? How many steps, thresholds and turns are in the way? What about at night, with the power out?

A tornado warning can give you only a few minutes. Our guide to [tornado watch vs warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/) explains what each alert means, and the practical point is the same: the shelter needs to be reachable quickly, by everyone, without help if possible. If one person needs another person's help to get in, plan for that, because it changes which unit makes sense and where it should go.

A shelter in the garage or attached to the house often wins on this front, since the route is short and sheltered from the weather. A shelter across the yard means a trip outside, over grass or gravel, possibly in rain or hail.

## Above-ground safe rooms: usually the easiest to enter

An [above-ground safe room](/above-ground-safe-rooms/) sits on the slab or foundation at floor level. That's the big advantage for accessibility: there are no stairs. The entry is a door at roughly ground or floor height, so the main questions are the threshold and the door width.

These units come in a range of sizes, and pricing in North Texas in 2026 runs about $4,500-$6,200 for a 4-6 person steel safe room, $6,500-$8,200 for 8-10 people, and $8,500-$11,500 for a large 12-16 person unit. A bigger footprint usually helps with turning room, which matters as much as the headcount.

Placement is the other lever. A safe room built into a garage corner, a closet or an interior room can keep the route short and flat. Our guide to a [safe room in the house](/blog/safe-room-in-house/) covers what's involved when the shelter is part of the home itself.

## Garage and in-floor units

A [garage floor shelter](/garage-floor-shelters/) is set into the garage slab, typically with a lid or hatch at floor level, and installed prices run about $4,800-$7,900. It keeps you under cover from the car to the shelter, which is a plus.

For accessibility, look closely at how you get down. In-floor units usually have stairs or a ladder below the lid. If stairs are a problem for the person who'd be using it, an in-floor unit may not be the right fit, unless the model has an entry that works for them. Ask to see how someone with limited mobility gets in and out, and how a lid that sits at floor level is opened from inside.

## Underground shelters: the stairs question

[Underground storm shelters](/underground-storm-shelters/) tend to be the hardest to make accessible, because you're going down. Slope-front models, with an entry on a slope or short stairs to a door, are more manageable than flat-top models with a hatch and a vertical drop, but both usually involve steps. Underground concrete slope-front units run about $5,200-$7,800, and flat-tops about $5,800-$8,800.

If anyone who'd use the shelter can't do stairs reliably, an underground unit is often the wrong choice, even if it fits the lot well. Our comparison of [above-ground vs underground shelters](/blog/above-ground-vs-underground-shelter/) breaks down the tradeoffs, and our guide to [how deep an underground storm shelter sits](/blog/how-deep-underground-storm-shelter/) explains why the entry design varies by model.

Older adults who can manage a few stairs on a good day may not manage them in a hurry, in the dark, or with a walker. Be realistic about the worst-case version of the trip.

## Door width and turning room

This is where accessibility gets specific. Two things decide whether a wheelchair or walker actually fits: the width of the door opening and the clear floor space inside.

On door width, ask about wider options. A 36-inch door is offered on some units. Confirm which models offer it, whether it changes the price, and whether the wider door is part of the tested assembly. A wider door that wasn't tested with the frame isn't a substitute for a tested one, and our guide to [storm shelter doors](/blog/storm-shelter-doors/) explains why the whole assembly matters.

Inside, consider:

- **Turning room.** A chair needs space to get in, turn and park. A tight shelter sized only for headcount may leave no room for that.
- **Where the chair ends up.** Will the person stay in the chair, transfer to a bench, or park the chair against a wall? Make sure there's a spot that doesn't block the door.
- **Door swing.** An in-swing door needs clear floor to open. An out-swing door doesn't, but debris can block it. Which is better depends on the layout.
- **Benches and shelves.** Fixed seating and storage can eat the floor space you need.
- **Walkers, oxygen and equipment.** Plan space for the gear that comes with the person.

Our [storm shelter size guide](/blog/storm-shelter-size-guide/) explains how footprint relates to capacity. For accessibility, treat the stated capacity as a ceiling and build in extra room.

## Ramps, thresholds and the approach

Even a flat entry can be a barrier if there's a lip at the door. Ask what the threshold height is and whether a ramp can be built or supplied. A short ramp from a driveway, patio or garage floor can make an above-ground unit practical.

Whatever you choose, think through the surface and slope: gravel, uneven concrete or a steep drive can stop a chair or walker. Check what's required, since adding a ramp or changing the pad may involve your city or county building department. Our guide to [storm shelter permits in Texas](/blog/storm-shelter-permits-texas/) explains where to start.

## Getting there fast: the whole plan

The shelter is one piece. The rest is the plan around it.

- **Get alerts early.** Wireless Emergency Alerts push tornado warnings to phones in the warned area, no app needed. A NOAA Weather Radio, with your county's SAME code, gives you another warning that works when cell service is down. See our guides to [tornado alerts on your phone](/blog/tornado-alerts-on-your-phone/) and [NOAA Weather Radio in Texoma](/blog/noaa-weather-radio-texoma/).
- **Move on the watch, not the warning, when it's hard to move.** If getting into the shelter takes extra time, don't wait for the warning. Consider heading to the shelter or a safe spot once a watch or a serious outlook is in place, based on your own judgment.
- **Practice the trip.** Time it. Do it once in daylight and once in the dark, with the person who'd actually use it.
- **Keep the path clear.** Nothing stored in the hallway, no cords across the floor, no gear blocking the door.
- **Stock the shelter.** Medications, a flashlight, water, a phone charger. Our [emergency kit guide](/blog/emergency-kit-storm-shelter/) and [storm shelter supplies list](/blog/storm-shelter-supplies/) are good starting points.
- **Plan for a helper.** If someone needs assistance, decide who does what, and who's the backup.

If your household includes a caregiver, a neighbor or a family member nearby, share the plan with them. Don't try to be a hero. If you can't get to the shelter in time, move to the safest place you can reach and follow guidance in our [safest place in a tornado](/blog/safest-place-in-a-tornado/) guide.

## Questions to ask about accessibility

| Ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Which models are step-free from the garage or yard? | Stairs are the main barrier |
| Is a 36-inch door available, and is it part of the tested assembly? | A wider opening helps chairs and walkers |
| What's the clear floor space inside, after benches and shelves? | Headcount capacity doesn't equal turning room |
| What's the threshold height, and can a ramp be added? | A small lip can stop a chair |
| Which way does the door swing, and how much room does that take? | Affects space and debris risk |
| How do I open and exit it from inside? | Must work without outside help |
| Can I see a demonstration or an example installed? | You should test it with the actual user |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the best storm shelter for someone in a wheelchair?

An above-ground safe room with a step-free entry is usually the easiest to use, especially one placed in a garage or inside the home. Look for a wide door, enough turning room inside and a low or ramped threshold. The best choice depends on your layout and who'll be using it.

### Can a wheelchair fit through a storm shelter door?

It depends on the door width. Standard shelter doors may be tight for a chair, and a 36-inch door is offered on some units. Ask whether a wider door is offered for the model you want, and confirm it's part of the tested assembly.

### Are underground storm shelters accessible?

Usually not without help. Most underground models involve steps or a ladder, and flat-top designs have a vertical drop through a hatch. Slope-front models are easier, but they still typically have stairs. If stairs are a problem, consider an above-ground or garage unit.

### How much room do I need inside for a wheelchair?

Enough to enter, turn or position the chair, and keep the door clear. A shelter sized only by headcount may not leave that room, so ask about the clear floor space and choose a larger footprint if needed. Treat the stated capacity as a maximum.

### What if I can't get to the shelter in time?

Plan for that before storm season. Consider moving earlier, once a watch is issued, place the shelter close to where you spend the most time, and arrange for a helper. If you can't reach it in time, get to the safest place you can and call 911 if you need emergency help.

### Does the Grayson County rebate cover accessible features?

The rebate is 50% of cost up to $3,000 for a shelter meeting FEMA P-320 and ICC 500, with pre-approval required before installation. Whether specific accessibility add-ons count is a question for the county program, so ask before you order. The program runs periodically and isn't always open.

# The Safest Place in a Tornado When Your Texas Home Has No Basement

Most homes in Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties sit on a slab, so "get to the basement" isn't an option here. The safest place in a tornado, in order, is a tested safe room or storm shelter, then a small interior room on the lowest floor with no windows, then whatever hallway or closet gives you the most walls between you and the outside. Where you rank below that depends on what kind of home you're in and how much warning you get.

## The Ranked List: Best to Worst

| Rank | Location | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tested safe room or storm shelter (FEMA P-320/P-361, ICC 500) | Built and tested to survive direct debris impact at 250 mph design wind speed |
| 2 | Small interior room, lowest floor, no windows (bathroom, closet, hallway) | Extra walls between you and flying debris; no glass |
| 3 | Interior hallway or room with windows covered by furniture or a mattress | Fewer walls, but still away from exterior glass |
| 4 | Vehicle, seatbelt on, head covered, parked away from trees and power lines | Only if you cannot reach a building in time |
| 5 | Ditch or low ground, lying flat, hands over head | Last resort only, if caught outside with no shelter or vehicle nearby |
| Avoid entirely | Mobile or manufactured home, garage, gym, big-box store, highway overpass | Wide roof spans collapse; overpasses funnel wind and offer no real cover |

A tested safe room is the only item on that list built and tested against the specific thing that kills people in a tornado: flying debris hitting a wall or door at high speed. Everything else on the list is a fallback, not a solution.

## Your Best Option: A Tested Safe Room or Storm Shelter

FEMA uses the phrase "near-absolute protection" for safe rooms built to its own criteria, FEMA P-320 for homes and FEMA P-361 for community and commercial buildings. Those aren't marketing lines. They describe a structure built to a specific standard, then tested against it, and the construction standard is ICC 500, the same one referenced by Texas building codes. The test that matters most is the debris impact test: a 15 lb piece of 2x4 lumber fired at a wall or door at 100 mph, and at a roof at 67 mph, to simulate what a tornado throws. Doors get tested as a full assembly too, frame, hinges and latch, not just the panel. None of that matters much if the door's hung wrong. Fit and anchoring count for as much as the shelter itself.

If your home has a safe room or storm shelter already, rated to those standards and correctly anchored for your soil, that's where you go the moment a warning is issued, no further decision needed.

If you don't have one yet, [above-ground safe rooms](/above-ground-safe-rooms/) are a common retrofit for slab homes in Texoma because they bolt into an existing garage or interior room without excavation. [Underground storm shelters](/underground-storm-shelters/) are the other main option, though installers have to check soil and drainage first: Grayson County's Blackland Prairie clay can shift and hold water, and a light shell can actually be pushed upward by saturated ground if it isn't properly anchored or ballasted. Either style, done right, meets the same P-320 and ICC 500 bar. Pricing depends on size and site conditions; the [storm shelter cost page](/storm-shelter-cost/) breaks down current ranges by shelter type.

## No Shelter Yet? The Next Best Place at Home

Without a safe room, NWS guidance is specific: get to the lowest floor, pick an interior room with no windows, and put as many walls as you can between yourself and the outside. In a typical Texoma slab home that usually means an interior bathroom, a closet under the stairs, or a central hallway. Small rooms are safer than large ones because their framing and roof span are shorter and more likely to hold.

Once you're in the room:

- Get under something sturdy if there's room, like a heavy table.
- Cover your head and neck with your arms, a helmet, a mattress or thick pillows. Head injuries from debris are a leading cause of tornado deaths, and it's also the one you can do the most about in the moment.
- Close interior doors between you and the rest of the house.
- Put on shoes if you have seconds to spare; broken glass afterward is a real hazard.
- Keep a phone with you, charged, with weather alerts on.

## What to Avoid

Some spaces feel like shelter but aren't:

- Windows and anything near them, including a room with a single exterior wall of glass.
- Rooms with a wide, unsupported roof span: garages, gyms, cafeterias, big-box retail floors. Long spans are exactly what fails first in high wind.
- Mobile and manufactured homes, even tied down. More on that below.
- Vehicles, unless you have no other option.
- Highway overpasses. Wind actually speeds up and funnels under an overpass; it is not a shelter and NWS advises against stopping there.
- A ditch, but only as an absolute last resort if you're caught outside with no building or vehicle within reach.

## In a Manufactured Home

Manufactured and mobile homes are not safe in a tornado, even when properly tied down. The structure and anchoring system aren't built to resist direct tornado wind loads or debris impact the way a site-built room or a rated safe room is. NWS guidance is to leave a mobile home before severe weather arrives and get to a sturdy building or a shelter, not to ride it out inside. If you live in a manufactured home in Texoma, that means knowing ahead of time where you'll go: a neighbor's safe room, a public building, or your own shelter if you've had one installed on the lot. Our [guide to storm shelters for mobile homes](/blog/mobile-home-storm-shelter/) covers what installers look at for anchoring a shelter next to a manufactured home site.

## In a Car

If a warning catches you driving and there's no building or shelter within a minute or two, stay in the car with your seatbelt on, get below window level, and cover your head with your arms or a blanket. Do not try to outrun a tornado, and do not stop under an overpass. If you can safely get to a sturdy building, do that instead of staying in the vehicle. A car offers some protection from flying debris but none from a direct hit or from being rolled, so it's a fallback, not a plan.

## At Work, School, or Church

Ask now, not during a warning, where the building's interior shelter area is. Schools and larger public buildings usually have a designated area, usually an interior hallway or room away from windows and away from wide-span spaces like gyms and cafeterias. Churches and small businesses often don't have a formal plan; if you run one, walk the building and pick the smallest, most interior, most windowless room now, and make sure staff know it. [Commercial and community safe rooms](/commercial-storm-shelters/) built to FEMA P-361 are the higher bar for a business, school or church that wants tested protection rather than "the best available room."

## Outdoors at Lake Texoma

Boaters, campers and anyone on the shoreline are in a tough spot because there's rarely a real shelter nearby. Get off the water and away from the shoreline at the first sign of a watch, not a warning; a tornado watch means conditions are favorable, and you want to already be moving toward shelter before a warning is issued. If you can reach a sturdy building, a restroom block or a marina office with solid walls is better than nothing. If you're caught in the open with no building or vehicle, lying flat in a low spot with your hands over your head is the last-resort option, not a plan you rely on. Anyone spending regular time at the lake during spring storm season should know in advance which nearby buildings they'd actually run to.

## Have a Plan Before the Warning

The time to decide where you're going is before the siren, not during it. A few minutes now saves confusion later. Walk your home and pick the safest available space if you don't have a shelter yet: interior, lowest floor, no windows, smallest reasonable footprint. Agree with your household on where everyone goes if a warning hits while you're apart, home, work, school, and if you don't have a rated safe room yet, find out which nearby home, business or public building does.

- **Stage supplies there.** Shoes, a couple of helmets or bike helmets for kids, a flashlight, and a battery radio. Our [storm shelter emergency kit checklist](/blog/emergency-kit-storm-shelter/) covers what to keep on hand whether you're using a rated shelter or a fallback room.
- **Turn on phone alerts.** Make sure Wireless Emergency Alerts are enabled and know the difference between a [tornado watch and a tornado warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/) so you're not deciding what the words mean while a storm is already on radar.

Grayson County's clay soil and slab-on-grade tradition are a big part of why so few Texas homes have basements in the first place; our [explainer on why Texas homes don't have basements](/blog/why-texas-homes-dont-have-basements/) goes into the soil and cost reasons if you're curious. It doesn't change the plan, though: without a basement, a tested safe room is the closest thing to it, and an interior windowless room is the fallback everyone in the house should already know by heart.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the safest place in a tornado if I don't have a basement or storm shelter?

The lowest floor of your home, in a small interior room with no windows, such as a bathroom, closet or central hallway. Put as many walls as you can between yourself and the outside and cover your head.

### Is a closet or bathroom really safer than the rest of the house?

Yes. Small interior rooms have shorter roof spans and more surrounding walls than larger rooms, which gives you more protection from collapsing structure and flying debris, especially compared to a room with exterior windows.

### Are mobile homes ever safe during a tornado?

No. NWS guidance is clear that manufactured and mobile homes are not safe in a tornado even when tied down. Leave for a sturdy building or a shelter before the storm arrives rather than sheltering inside.

### Should I shelter under a highway overpass if I'm caught driving?

No. Wind speeds up and funnels under an overpass, and NWS advises against using one as shelter. If you can't reach a sturdy building, stay in your vehicle with your seatbelt on and your head covered below window level.

### What makes a storm shelter different from just a strong interior room?

A storm shelter or safe room built to FEMA P-320 or P-361 criteria is designed and tested to ICC 500, including a debris impact test that simulates a 15 lb 2x4 hitting the walls and door at 100 mph. An interior room in a standard home hasn't been engineered or tested against that kind of impact; it's the best fallback, not a tested structure.

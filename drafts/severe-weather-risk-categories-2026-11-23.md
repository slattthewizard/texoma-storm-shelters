# Enhanced Risk Severe Weather Meaning: SPC Outlook Categories 1 to 5

You check the forecast and see "enhanced risk of severe weather" in your area. Is that a big deal, or just another storm day? The labels sound similar, and it's easy to mix them up with watches and warnings. They're different tools with different jobs.

Here's what the enhanced risk severe weather meaning really is, how the five categories compare, and what each one should mean for how you plan your day in Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties.

## Where the categories come from

The Storm Prediction Center, based in Norman, Oklahoma, issues the convective outlook. It looks ahead one to several days and ranks the potential for severe thunderstorms on a five-level scale:

1. Marginal
2. Slight
3. Enhanced
4. Moderate
5. High

The categories reflect two things together: how widespread severe storms are likely to be, and how intense they could get. So a higher category can mean more storms, stronger storms, or both. The Storm Prediction Center also issues tornado watches. Local National Weather Service offices, including Fort Worth for Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties, issue tornado warnings. Across the lake in Bryan, Marshall and Love counties in Oklahoma, the local office is NWS Norman.

## The five categories at a glance

| Level | Name | What it generally signals | What your household should do |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Marginal | Isolated severe storms possible, usually limited in coverage or intensity | Check alerts, know your plan, no big changes |
| 2 | Slight | Scattered severe storms possible | Review your shelter spot, charge phones, keep alerts on |
| 3 | Enhanced | Numerous severe storms possible, some may be intense | Pay close attention, plan your day around the storm window |
| 4 | Moderate | Widespread severe storms likely, some may be intense | Be ready to shelter early, adjust travel and events |
| 5 | High | Widespread, intense severe storms expected | Treat it as a day to change plans and stay close to shelter |

## What each level should mean for your plan

**Marginal (1).** Severe storms are possible but not widespread. Most places won't see anything severe. It's still a day to know your plan, since a single storm can cause real damage. Make sure your phone is charged and alerts are on.

**Slight (2).** Scattered severe storms are possible. This is a good time to double-check your shelter spot, your flashlight, and where the kids and pets will go. Our [storm shelter prep](/blog/storm-shelter-prep/) page is a helpful checklist.

**Enhanced (3).** This is the level people often underestimate. It signals a more organized threat with more storms, and some may be strong. If you're traveling, scheduling outdoor events, or heading to the lake, look at the timing of the storm window. Keep your phone nearby and be ready to act the moment a watch or warning appears. It also helps to review [tornado watch vs warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/) so you know exactly what triggers action.

**Moderate (4).** Widespread severe storms are likely, and some could be intense. Move non-essential outdoor plans, and keep your shelter ready and accessible. Don't leave the household split in different places during the storm window if you can avoid it.

**High (5).** This is the top of the scale and rare. It means widespread, intense severe weather is expected, which can include long-track tornadoes. Treat it as a day to stay home or near a safe place, take watches seriously, and shelter early.

None of the categories tell you the exact time or spot a tornado will form. A Marginal day can still produce a damaging storm somewhere, and a High day doesn't mean every neighborhood sees a tornado. The categories are a planning aid, not a promise.

## Outlook, watch and warning: three different things

People often mix these up, so here's the difference.

- **Outlook.** The forecast for the days ahead. It tells you where severe weather could happen and how significant it might be. It's time to prepare, not to shelter.
- **Watch.** Conditions are favorable for severe storms or tornadoes in the area. Stay alert and be ready to move.
- **Warning.** Severe weather is occurring or imminent. Take shelter now.

Think of it like this: an outlook is "this could be a stormy day," a watch is "storms are possible now, be ready," and a warning is "it's happening, go." If you get a tornado warning, you don't need to check what category the day is. Go to your shelter or safest place.

## Day-of habits that make any category easier

- Check the outlook in the morning on days with storms in the forecast.
- Keep your phone charged. Wireless Emergency Alerts send tornado warnings to phones in the warned area with no app or sign-up. See [tornado alerts on your phone](/blog/tornado-alerts-on-your-phone/).
- Consider a NOAA Weather Radio, which can alert you even if cell service drops. Your SAME county code lets it alert only for your area: Grayson 048181, Fannin 048147, Cooke 048097. More in our guide to [NOAA Weather Radio in Texoma](/blog/noaa-weather-radio-texoma/).
- Know where everyone in the family will go, and practice it. Our [tornado drill for kids](/blog/tornado-drill-for-kids/) article helps.
- Plan for pets and a few supplies. See [emergency kit for a storm shelter](/blog/emergency-kit-storm-shelter/).
- Don't plan long drives through the storm window. If you're caught on the road, see [what to do in a tornado while driving](/blog/what-to-do-tornado-while-driving/).

## When tornadoes are most likely here

North Texas has tornadoes in every month. Spring, roughly March through June, is the main peak, with a smaller bump in the fall. That means an Enhanced or Moderate risk in April or May deserves extra attention, but an unexpected storm in October or December can be dangerous too. Read more in [Texas tornado season](/blog/texas-tornado-season/).

## Where a shelter fits in

On a category 3 or higher day, the question many families ask is where they'd actually go. Many Texas homes sit on slabs with no basement, so there's no obvious below-grade spot. A purpose-built shelter solves that. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does enhanced risk mean for severe weather?

Enhanced is level 3 of 5 on the Storm Prediction Center's convective outlook. It means numerous severe storms are possible, and some could be intense. It's a day to keep alerts on, watch the storm timing, and be ready to act if a watch or warning appears.

### Is an enhanced risk a bad day for tornadoes?

It can be. An enhanced risk doesn't guarantee a tornado, but it signals a more organized severe threat than marginal or slight. Review your shelter plan and keep your phone nearby.

### What's the difference between a risk category and a tornado watch?

The risk category is part of a forecast outlook that looks ahead, often a day or more. A tornado watch is issued by the Storm Prediction Center when conditions are favorable for tornadoes right now in a specific area. A warning, issued by your local NWS office, means a tornado is occurring or imminent.

### Which risk level is the most dangerous?

High, level 5, is the top category and is used rarely. It signals widespread, intense severe weather. But severe weather can cause real harm on lower-risk days too, so don't ignore a marginal or slight outlook.

### Who issues the severe weather risk categories?

The Storm Prediction Center in Norman, Oklahoma issues the convective outlook. For Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties, the local forecast office is NWS Fort Worth, which issues the tornado warnings. Bryan, Marshall and Love counties in Oklahoma are covered by NWS Norman.

### Should I change my plans on an enhanced risk day?

Look at the timing first. Many enhanced days have a narrow window when storms are most likely. Avoid long drives and outdoor events during that window, and keep a plan to get to shelter quickly. If a warning is issued, take shelter immediately.

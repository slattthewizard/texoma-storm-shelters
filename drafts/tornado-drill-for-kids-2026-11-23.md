# Tornado Drill for Kids: A Calm Family Practice Plan

Kids pick up on your mood faster than they pick up on facts. If a tornado warning sends you running around the house with a pale face, they'll remember the fear far more than the plan. If you've practiced, and it feels almost boring, they'll follow the plan.

That's the goal of a family tornado drill: make it so routine that when a warning comes, everyone already knows what to do. Here's how to run one, how to talk to kids about tornadoes without scaring them, and what to say when it's the real thing.

## Start with a calm conversation, not a drill

Before you practice, talk. Pick a relaxed time, not during a storm and not at bedtime. Keep it short, honest, and matter-of-fact.

A few approaches that work:

- **Explain it like any other safety rule.** Fire drills, seat belts, bike helmets. A tornado plan is the same kind of thing: something grown-ups prepare for so nobody has to worry.
- **Use true, simple words.** "A tornado is a strong, spinning wind that can come from a big storm. We have a plan to stay safe, and we're going to practice it." You don't need to describe damage or what could go wrong.
- **Answer the questions they ask, and stop there.** Younger kids often want to know where you'll go and who's coming. Older kids may want to know how tornadoes form. Follow their lead instead of giving a lecture.
- **Don't promise what you can't promise.** "We'll always be safe" isn't something you can guarantee. "We have a plan, and we're going to follow it together" is true.
- **Watch for worry.** If a child is anxious, let them hold a comfort item, practice a few extra times, or help with a job. Having a role often calms nerves.

Avoid scary stories, graphic news footage, and overheard adult worry. Kids sense more than you'd think. If they've seen something frightening on TV or heard it at school, ask what they think happened and clear up what's wrong.

## Know the words: watch versus warning

Older kids can learn the difference between a watch and a warning, and it helps them understand why you act when you do. A tornado watch means conditions are right for tornadoes to develop in the area, so it's time to be ready. A tornado warning means a tornado has been spotted or indicated by radar, and it's time to take shelter now. Our guide to [tornado watch versus warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/) explains the difference in plain language.

For younger kids, you can simplify: "A watch means get ready. A warning means go to our safe spot."

## Pick a spot and a backup

Your drill needs a destination. If you have a storm shelter, that's it. If you don't, choose the best interior space you have: a small room on the lowest floor, away from windows, like a bathroom, closet, or interior hallway. Our guide to the [safest place in a tornado](/blog/safest-place-in-a-tornado/) explains how to choose one, and our article on [safe rooms in a house](/blog/safe-room-in-house/) covers options if you're thinking about building one.

A few tips for a family with kids:

- **Walk it, don't just point at it.** Have each child walk to the spot, sit down, and see what it feels like.
- **Pick a backup.** If a child is in the bedroom or backyard when a warning hits, where do they go? If you're at a grandparent's house, where would you go there?
- **Check it's usable.** Is the closet jammed with boxes? Can everyone fit? A small shelter fills quickly, so see our [size guide](/blog/storm-shelter-size-guide/) if you're planning one.
- **Keep it simple for little ones.** "Our safe spot is the hall closet" is easier to remember than a list of rules.

## Run the drill: a simple routine

Keep drills short, ideally five to ten minutes, and run them when everyone's home. Here's a routine that works for most families.

1. **Announce it.** "This is a tornado drill. Pretend we just heard a warning." Use a signal or word you'll use in real life, so the cue becomes familiar.
2. **Stop what you're doing.** TV off, game paused, shoes on.
3. **Move to the safe spot.** Walk, don't run, and no stopping for toys.
4. **Get in position.** Sit low, head down, hands over the back of your neck. A blanket or mattress over everyone helps against debris. Helmets are a good idea for kids and adults alike.
5. **Count heads.** One adult checks that everyone's there.
6. **Wait quietly.** Practice staying put for a few minutes. Read a book, play a quiet game, sing something.
7. **Debrief.** Talk about what went well and what to change. Let the kids say what felt confusing.

Time yourself if you like, but don't turn it into a race that stresses anyone. Once the routine is familiar, try a surprise drill at a different time of day. A night drill, with lights off and kids in their pajamas, is especially useful, since a warning can come in the middle of the night. Our article on [nighttime tornadoes](/blog/nighttime-tornadoes/) explains why it's worth practicing.

Run drills a few times a year, with one before spring, since North Texas has tornadoes in every month but spring is the main peak. A fall refresher is smart too.

## Who grabs what: give everyone a job

Assigning jobs keeps panic down and gives kids a feeling of control. Adjust to your family's ages:

| Person | Job |
|---|---|
| Parent or adult 1 | Carries the youngest child, grabs phone and charger |
| Parent or adult 2 | Brings the kit, flashlight, and shoes, then opens the door of the safe spot |
| Older child (about 10 and up) | Helps a younger sibling, brings the pet carrier or leash |
| Younger child | Brings their comfort item and picks the storytime book |
| Pet | Goes with the person who's closest, in a carrier or on a leash |

No job should require a kid to do something heavy or risky. Anything that slows them down, like hunting for a toy, should be cut from the routine. If someone isn't home when a warning hits, the plan should say what they do there, not rush to get back.

## Pets: a part of the plan

Pets are family, and kids will worry about them. Include them in the drill so everyone knows what happens.

- Keep a leash and carrier or crate by the safe spot or near the door.
- Assign one person to the pet, and a backup person.
- Practice with the actual animal. Some pets run and hide when they're stressed, so practice what you'd do.
- Keep a small bag with food, water, and any medication in your kit.

Our guide to [pet safety in a storm shelter](/blog/pet-safety-storm-shelter/) goes into more detail.

## A go-bag and comfort items for kids

A family kit helps, but kids benefit from a small bag of their own too. Let them help pack it, since that gives them a role.

- **A comfort item.** A stuffed animal, blanket, or small toy.
- **Something quiet to do.** A book, cards, or a small puzzle.
- **A flashlight.** A small one they can hold, with spare batteries.
- **Shoes.** Sturdy ones, kept near the safe spot, because debris and broken glass are real hazards.
- **A snack and water bottle.** Simple and non-messy.
- **A helmet.** A bike or sports helmet is a sensible addition.
- **Any medications or special needs items.** Keep these in the main kit, with notes for babysitters.

The adult kit should include a phone charger or battery pack, a first aid basics pack, and a battery or hand-crank radio. ## School and daycare drills

Schools run tornado drills, usually in a hallway or interior room with kids crouched against the wall. Ask your child what they do, and compare it to your home routine. Having similar steps in both places makes things easier to remember.

It's also worth asking the school or daycare:

- Where do the kids go during a warning, and how are they accounted for?
- How will parents be contacted, and when will kids be released afterward?
- What's the plan for kids with special needs?

Remind your child that during a school drill or warning, their teacher is in charge, and they should follow instructions even if they wish they could be with you. That's a hard thing for a younger child to hear, so say it in a reassuring way: "Your teacher knows what to do, and I know you'll be okay."

## What to say during a real warning

When it's real, the script matters. Keep your voice steady, your words short, and your tone like the drill.

- "We heard a tornado warning. It's time to go to our safe spot. Let's go."
- "Shoes on. Grab your comfort item."
- "Get low. Cover your head. I'm right here."
- "We're doing exactly what we practiced."

Then stay put until you get an all-clear. A warning has an end time, and you should wait until it passes, using a weather radio or the alerts on your phone. Wireless Emergency Alerts push tornado warnings to your phone with no app or sign-up, and a NOAA Weather Radio with your county code (Grayson 048181, Fannin 048147, Cooke 048097) is a good backup. See our guides to [tornado alerts on your phone](/blog/tornado-alerts-on-your-phone/) and [NOAA Weather Radio in Texoma](/blog/noaa-weather-radio-texoma/).

If you're in a car, don't try to outrun a tornado that's close. Our article on [what to do if a tornado threatens while you're driving](/blog/what-to-do-tornado-while-driving/) explains your options. Kids in the back seat should hear a calm explanation of what you're doing.

After the warning passes, talk about it. Ask how they felt, answer questions, and check for injuries. If anyone is hurt, call 911. For what comes next, see our guide on [what to do after a tornado](/blog/after-a-tornado/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should we practice a tornado drill at home?

A few times a year is a good rhythm, with one before spring since that's the main peak in North Texas. Add a fall refresher, and run a night drill at least once so kids know what to do in the dark.

### How do I explain tornadoes to a young child without scaring them?

Keep it simple and true: a tornado is a strong, spinning wind from a big storm, and your family has a plan to stay safe. Skip graphic details, answer only what they ask, and give them a job in the drill so they feel capable.

### What should kids bring to the safe spot?

A comfort item, sturdy shoes, a small flashlight, and something quiet to do. A helmet is a good idea too. Keep heavier supplies, like water, a first aid kit, and a radio, in the adult kit so kids aren't carrying much.

### What if a child is at school or daycare during a warning?

Teachers and staff follow the school's tornado plan, so your child should do what the adult in charge says. Ask the school where kids shelter, how parents are contacted, and when children are released, and tell your child you trust the teacher.

### What if my child is too scared to practice?

Go slower. Start with a conversation, let them hold a comfort item, and let them help pick the safe spot. Keep drills short, praise what they do well, and avoid surprise drills until they feel comfortable.

### Should the whole family stay together during a warning?

Yes, if everyone's home, go to the safe spot together and count heads. If someone is away, they should follow the plan where they are, such as going to the school's shelter area, and not try to drive home during the warning.

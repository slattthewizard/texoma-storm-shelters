# The Enhanced Fujita Scale: EF0 to EF5, Explained for Homeowners

When a tornado hits, the news soon gives it a number: EF1, EF3, sometimes EF4. That number comes from the Enhanced Fujita scale, and it's often misunderstood. It isn't a forecast, it isn't a measurement of the funnel's size, and it isn't assigned while the storm is happening. It's an estimate of wind speed based on the damage left behind. This guide explains how the scale works, what each rating looks like in practice, and what the numbers mean for your house and for a tested shelter.

## What the scale is and where it came from

The Enhanced Fujita scale has been used in the United States since 2007. It replaced the original Fujita scale, which rated tornadoes by general damage descriptions. The newer version ties damage to specific building types and materials, with the goal of more consistent ratings.

The scale has six ratings, EF0 through EF5, each tied to an estimated range of wind speeds. Those speeds are 3-second gust estimates. That's a detail worth remembering: the numbers aren't measured by an instrument at the scene. They're estimated from what the wind did to structures and trees.

| Rating | Estimated wind (3-second gust) |
|---|---|
| EF0 | 65 to 85 mph |
| EF1 | 86 to 110 mph |
| EF2 | 111 to 135 mph |
| EF3 | 136 to 165 mph |
| EF4 | 166 to 200 mph |
| EF5 | Over 200 mph |

## What each rating typically looks like

Damage descriptions below are typical, not guaranteed. A tornado's rating comes from the most severe damage found, and the same wind can do different things to a well built house and a poorly built one.

**EF0 (65 to 85 mph).** Light damage. Shingles peel off, gutters and siding get damaged, branches break, and shallow rooted trees may be pushed over. Weak by tornado standards, but it can still hurt someone outdoors.

**EF1 (86 to 110 mph).** Moderate damage. Roof surfaces can be badly stripped, mobile homes can be overturned or severely damaged, windows break and exterior doors and garage doors can fail. Trees snap, and moving vehicles can be pushed off the road.

**EF2 (111 to 135 mph).** Considerable damage. Roofs can be torn from well built frame houses, mobile homes are destroyed, large trees are snapped or uprooted, and light objects become dangerous missiles. Our guide on [mobile home storm shelters](/blog/mobile-home-storm-shelter/) covers why manufactured homes are so vulnerable at the lower end of the scale.

**EF3 (136 to 165 mph).** Severe damage. Entire stories of well built houses can be destroyed, large buildings like shopping centers and schools can suffer major damage, trains can be overturned, and heavy vehicles are lifted off the ground. Trees are stripped and debarked.

**EF4 (166 to 200 mph).** Devastating damage. Well built houses can be leveled, structures with weak foundations can be blown some distance, and cars are thrown. Debris fields can be enormous.

**EF5 (over 200 mph).** Incredible damage. Strong frame houses can be swept off their foundations and carried away, high rise buildings can have major structural deformation, and the debris can be carried long distances. EF5 tornadoes are rare, but the scale exists so the worst cases have a category.

Remember that damage depends on construction quality as much as on wind. Poor anchoring, weak roof connections, and missing bolts mean a house can fail at lower speeds than the scale suggests. That's why a rating describes the damage found, and why two houses on the same street can fare very differently.

## How a tornado gets its rating

A rating isn't assigned during the storm. After the tornado, meteorologists from the National Weather Service go to the area and survey the damage. For Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties, that's the Fort Worth forecast office. For southern Oklahoma across the lake, it's the Norman office.

The survey process looks like this:

1. **Walk or drive the path.** Surveyors follow the track, photographing and documenting damage along the way, and sometimes use drone or aerial imagery.
2. **Match damage to indicators.** The scale uses a list of damage indicators, such as small homes, schools, barns, and trees, with degrees of damage for each. A surveyor picks the indicator and the degree that fits what they see.
3. **Estimate the wind.** Each degree of damage has an expected wind speed range. The surveyor considers construction quality, which can raise or lower the estimate.
4. **Assign the rating by the worst damage.** The tornado gets the highest rating supported by credible damage along the path.

That process explains a few things people find confusing. Ratings can change after the first reports, as more damage is found. A tornado crossing open fields may get a lower rating than it deserves because there was nothing for it to damage. And ratings are estimates, so two surveyors can sometimes reasonably differ.

The rating also says nothing about the size of the funnel, how long it was on the ground, or how far it traveled. A narrow EF3 and a wide EF3 get the same number, and a long track EF1 can do more total damage than a short EF2.

## What an EF rating means for your house

If you live in a conventional wood frame house on a slab, the scale is a useful reality check. At EF2 and above, the damage descriptions include roofs lost on well built houses. At EF3 and above, whole stories can go. A hallway or closet inside such a house is better than a window, but it isn't built to resist that kind of force or the debris it carries.

Many Texas homes are slab on grade with no basement, which removes the most protective option you'd have in other parts of the country. Our guide on [why Texas homes don't have basements](/blog/why-texas-homes-dont-have-basements/) explains the reasons, including clay soils. If you're deciding where to shelter inside the house, our guide on the [safest place in a tornado](/blog/safest-place-in-a-tornado/) covers it.

An EF rating after a storm is also not a promise about what will happen to your home next time. A weaker tornado can still destroy a poorly connected roof, and a stronger one can miss you entirely. You can't plan around a number. You plan around the possibility of a bad one, and you do that by having a tested place to go.

## Why shelters are designed to 250 mph

You'll notice that the top of the Enhanced Fujita scale is "over 200 mph," and that residential storm shelters are designed to a higher number. Under ICC 500 and FEMA P-320 criteria, the design wind speed for all of North Texas is 250 mph. That's well above the EF5 threshold.

The gap is intentional. Design standards add a margin because the wind speeds in the scale are estimates, because the pressure, uplift and debris loads on a structure are more complicated than a single number, and because a shelter has one job: to hold up in the worst case so people inside survive. Our guides on [understanding tornado wind loads](/blog/understanding-tornado-wind-loads/) and [EF5 tornado shelter design](/blog/ef5-tornado-shelter-design/) go into the engineering, and [FEMA P-320 standards](/blog/fema-p-320-storm-shelter-standards/) explains the criteria in more detail.

Wind speed is only half of the test. A shelter also has to resist debris. The standard tests walls, roof and door with a 15 lb 2x4 fired at 100 mph on a horizontal trajectory. That matters because flying debris is a major cause of injury in a tornado, and a shelter that survives wind but not the debris isn't doing its job. Our guide on [ICC 500 safe room requirements](/blog/icc-500-safe-room-requirements/) walks through the standard.

## House rating versus tested shelter

Here's the practical comparison. A house gets its rating after the fact, based on how it held up. A tested shelter is designed and tested before the storm, to a standard well above the top of the scale.

- A house is built to ordinary building codes for wind and weight, not to withstand a tornado. A rating tells you what a tornado did to it.
- A properly designed and installed shelter is built to FEMA P-320 and tested to ICC 500 criteria. Remember that FEMA does not certify or approve products or installers. What you want is a shelter built to those criteria, with test documentation for the exact model and door. Our guide on [storm shelter testing and certification](/blog/storm-shelter-testing-certification/) explains what to ask for.
- A shelter only works if it's anchored and installed correctly. A tested design that isn't installed to its specifications isn't a tested shelter.

Grayson County has run a safe room rebate through FEMA hazard mitigation grants that covers 50 percent of cost up to $3,000. It runs periodically and requires pre-approval before installation, so see our [rebate guide](/blog/grayson-county-storm-shelter-rebate/) first. If you live outside Grayson County, ask your county emergency management office whether a program is open.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the wind speeds for each Enhanced Fujita rating?

The estimated 3-second gust ranges are EF0 at 65 to 85 mph, EF1 at 86 to 110 mph, EF2 at 111 to 135 mph, EF3 at 136 to 165 mph, EF4 at 166 to 200 mph, and EF5 over 200 mph. These are estimates drawn from damage, not instrument readings at the tornado.

### How is a tornado's EF rating decided?

National Weather Service meteorologists survey the damage after the storm and match it to a list of damage indicators, like houses, schools and trees. Each degree of damage has an expected wind range, and the tornado is rated by the most severe damage supported by the evidence.

### Can a tornado's EF rating change after it's first announced?

Yes. Early ratings are preliminary, and more damage found during a full survey can raise or lower them. Ratings also depend on what the tornado hit, so one that crossed open land may be rated lower than its true strength.

### Why are shelters designed for 250 mph if EF5 starts at 200 mph?

The standard builds in a margin. The scale gives estimated wind speeds, while real loads on a structure include pressure, uplift and flying debris. ICC 500 and FEMA P-320 set the residential design wind speed at 250 mph for North Texas so the shelter holds in the worst case.

### Does an EF rating tell me if my house could survive?

Not by itself. The rating describes what damage was found after a storm, and construction quality matters a great deal. A house built to ordinary code isn't designed for tornado winds, and a tested shelter is built for far higher loads.

### Is an EF0 or EF1 tornado safe to ignore?

No. Lower rated tornadoes can still strip roofs, break windows, overturn mobile homes and throw debris. Take cover for any tornado warning, regardless of the rating you expect.

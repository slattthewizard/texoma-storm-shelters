# Tornado Alerts on Your Phone: How to Set Them Up and Where They Fall Short

Most of us hear about a tornado warning from the thing in our pocket. That's a real improvement over the old days of relying on a siren or a TV interruption. But a phone alert is only useful if it's switched on, if the phone is within reach, and if you're awake to notice it. This guide covers how phone alerts work, how to check yours, what backup tools are worth adding, and why you shouldn't make your phone your only warning.

## How Wireless Emergency Alerts work

Wireless Emergency Alerts, usually shortened to WEA, are short messages sent by authorities to phones in a specific area. For tornadoes, the National Weather Service issues the warning and the alert goes out to phones in the warned area. You don't download an app or sign up. If your phone is on, has a signal, and the alert setting is enabled, it can receive them.

A few details help explain what you'll see:

- **It's location based.** The alert goes to phones near the warned area, not to everyone in the county. That's why your phone may buzz while a phone a few miles outside the warned area stays quiet.
- **It's loud and different.** Alerts use a distinct tone and vibration, and they show up even if the phone is locked. Most phones play the sound even when set to silent, though some settings can change that.
- **It's brief.** The message gives you the basics: a tornado warning is in effect, the area, and a call to take shelter. It isn't a detailed forecast.
- **It's not a replacement for watching conditions.** It tells you a warning was issued. It doesn't tell you where the storm is moving right now.

If you've ever wondered how a warning differs from a watch, our guide on [tornado watch vs warning](/blog/tornado-watch-vs-warning/) covers it. The short version: a watch means conditions are right for tornadoes, and a warning means one is happening or about to. The phone alert is meant for the second one.

## Check that alerts are turned on

This takes about two minutes, and most people haven't done it. Menu names change between phone models and software versions, so use these as a guide and search your settings for "alerts" or "emergency" if you don't see them right away.

**On an iPhone:** open Settings, go to Notifications, and scroll to the bottom. You'll find a section called Government Alerts. Check that Emergency Alerts is turned on. Some regions also show Public Safety Alerts there.

**On an Android phone:** open Settings and look for Notifications, or search for "emergency alerts" or "wireless emergency alerts." The exact location depends on the manufacturer. Make sure severe alerts are enabled, and check whether your phone lets you change the alert sound or vibration.

Some other things to check while you're there:

- Make sure the phone isn't in Airplane mode overnight.
- If you use a Focus mode or Do Not Disturb on iPhone, or a similar scheduled quiet mode on Android, find out how it treats emergency alerts on your specific phone. Government and emergency alerts are generally designed to come through, but settings differ and it's worth testing rather than assuming.
- Keep your phone's software reasonably up to date. Alert behavior improves over time.
- If you've turned off some alert categories in the past, look again. Some people disable everything after an annoying alert and forget they did it.

Not everyone's phone will show the same options. If you can't find the setting, your carrier's support page or the phone maker's help site will tell you where it lives on your model.

## Add a local backup, because one source is a single point of failure

WEA is the baseline. Adding one or two local sources gives you more detail and a second chance to notice.

**A local weather app or TV station app.** Many local TV stations serving the Dallas/Fort Worth and Sherman/Ada areas offer apps that send push alerts when a warning is issued for your location, and often include live radar and the forecasters' commentary. Pick one or two and turn on location based warning alerts. More than that and you'll start ignoring the notifications.

**The National Weather Service itself.** The NWS Fort Worth office covers Grayson, Fannin and Cooke counties, and posts forecasts and warnings on its website and social media accounts. The Storm Prediction Center in Norman, Oklahoma, issues the tornado watches. If you want the source, that's where it starts. Across the lake, Bryan, Marshall and Love counties in Oklahoma are covered by the NWS Norman office, so if you spend time on both sides, it's worth knowing both.

**County and city alert systems.** Many cities and counties run their own opt-in notification systems for emergencies. Ask your city or county emergency management office what's available for your address. These can be useful for local updates after a storm, such as road closures.

Remember that every phone based tool shares the same weak points: the phone, its battery, and the network. Stacking five apps on one dead phone gets you nothing.

## Why a phone alone isn't enough

Phone alerts are good. They're not enough. Here's where they commonly fail.

**You're asleep and the phone is across the room.** Tornadoes in North Texas can happen at night, and the dark hours are when warnings are easiest to miss. A phone charging in the kitchen won't wake you in the back bedroom. Our guide on [nighttime tornadoes](/blog/nighttime-tornadoes/) goes through why this matters.

**The battery is dead.** It sounds trivial, but it's an easy way to miss an alert. Storms often take out power, and a phone that's at 8 percent when the warning comes might not last through a long evening of use.

**Do Not Disturb or silent settings get in the way.** Even if alerts are designed to break through, a phone left face down in a bag, or one with the sound off and no vibration, may still go unnoticed.

**Cell service gets congested or drops.** When thousands of people use their phones at once, or a tower loses power, delivery can be delayed. Alerts aren't guaranteed to arrive instantly or at all.

**You're somewhere with no signal.** Rural stretches of Fannin and Cooke counties and areas around the lake may have patchy coverage. If your phone has no signal, it can't receive the alert.

**You didn't read it.** An alert that wakes you for the third time in a month starts to feel routine. Treat every tornado warning as real until proven otherwise.

This is why layers matter. A [NOAA weather radio](/blog/noaa-weather-radio-texoma/) with battery backup works independently of your phone and cell network, and it's built to wake you up. A phone plus a radio covers most of the failures above.

## Sirens are for people outside

Outdoor warning sirens get blamed for not waking people up, but that's not what they're for. They're designed to alert people who are outdoors, such as someone at a ball field, working in a yard, or out on the lake. Inside a closed house with the air conditioning running, a siren may be faint or inaudible, and wind direction and distance change how well it carries.

Don't count on a siren to tell you whether to take cover. If you hear one, take it seriously and check your phone, radio or TV for details. If you don't hear one, that doesn't mean there's no warning. Sirens can fail, and a warning may be issued for your area while the nearest siren is out of earshot.

## What to do the moment the alert goes off

An alert is a prompt to act, not to investigate. A good plan is simple and rehearsed.

1. **Get to your shelter or safest spot now.** Don't step outside to look for the tornado.
2. **Take your phone, shoes, and anything that protects your head.** Keep these near where you sleep so you aren't hunting for them.
3. **Bring your radio and a flashlight if you have a shelter.** Our [emergency kit for a storm shelter](/blog/emergency-kit-storm-shelter/) lists what's worth keeping inside.
4. **Stay put until the warning expires or officials say it's clear.** A second storm can follow the first, and a radio or phone alert will tell you when the threat has passed.

If you're not sure where your safest spot is, read our guide to the [safest place in a tornado](/blog/safest-place-in-a-tornado/). Many Texas homes are slab-on-grade with no basement, which limits your options, so planning ahead matters. If you're driving when the alert hits, our guide on [what to do in a tornado while driving](/blog/what-to-do-tornado-while-driving/) covers it.

Notice what that list doesn't include: waiting for a second alert, checking social media first, or going outside to see if it's real. The point of a warning is the time it buys you. Seconds and minutes spent confirming are time not spent getting safe. Warning lead times vary and some are short, so the goal is to already know where you're going.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an app to get tornado alerts on my phone?

No. Wireless Emergency Alerts are sent to phones in the warned area without an app or sign-up, as long as emergency alerts are enabled in your settings. Local weather apps add more detail, like radar, but they aren't required for the basic alert.

### Will a tornado warning alert come through if my phone is on silent?

Emergency alerts are designed to sound even when the phone is silenced, but behavior depends on your phone and settings. Don't assume. Check your emergency alert settings, and test how your phone handles quiet modes like Do Not Disturb.

### Why didn't my phone alert me when my neighbor's did?

Alerts are location based, so a phone near the edge of a warned area may not receive one. Delays and spotty signal can also play a part. This is one reason to have a second source, such as a weather radio or a local weather app.

### Is a weather app better than the built-in alert?

They do different things. The built-in alert is automatic and needs no setup. A local weather or TV station app can give you radar and forecaster updates in addition. Using one of each gives you the best coverage, but neither replaces a battery powered weather radio.

### Are outdoor sirens meant to wake me up at night?

No. Sirens are intended to warn people who are outside. Inside a closed house, they may be hard to hear. Rely on your phone, a NOAA weather radio, or local TV for warnings while indoors.

### How much warning will I get from a phone alert?

It varies. Some tornado warnings give several minutes, and some give very little. The National Weather Service can't promise a set amount of time. That's why you should know exactly where you'll shelter before any alert arrives.
